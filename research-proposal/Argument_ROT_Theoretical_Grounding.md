## 1. Thesis of the argument

This study is grounded in **Resource Orchestration Theory (ROT)** because the research problem is not *whether* SMEs possess FinTech resources but *how* they convert externally available, non-rare digital financial services into cross-border internationalisation capabilities. ROT supplies the **managerial mechanism** the FinTech-internationalisation literature lacks: the structuring–bundling–leveraging processes through which resource endowments become value-creating capabilities (Sirmon, Hitt, & Ireland, 2007; Sirmon, Hitt, Ireland, & Gilbert, 2011). Applied to this phenomenon, the mechanism is theorised as **FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO)**. ROT does **not** define the internationalisation outcomes to be explained; International New Venture (INV) theory supplies that architecture. The FOI Framework therefore **extends** ROT into a new domain — externally sourced FinTech resources deployed for cross-border expansion in institutionally thin emerging markets — rather than merely relabelling adoption or digital capability.

## 2. The problem ROT solves: from possession to configuration

### 2.1 RBV's managerial silence

The resource-based view (RBV) relocates sustained competitive advantage from industry position to firm heterogeneity in resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable (Barney, 1991). Its enduring limitation is **managerial silence**: resources do not deploy themselves. Two firms with identical endowments can achieve radically different outcomes, and canonical RBV cannot explain why.

This limitation is not peripheral to the present study — it **is** the study's problem in miniature. FinTech resources are available to all firms in an ecosystem. If possession conferred advantage, all adopters would internationalise alike. They do not. The empirical literature therefore faces a puzzle: association between FinTech and internationalisation is increasingly established, yet **heterogeneity among similar adopters** remains unexplained (Anwar, Li, Al-Omush, & Al-Nimer, 2023; Bargoni, Ferraris, Vilamová, & Wan Hussain, 2024; Hajizamani, Zakery, & Bonyadi Naieni, 2025; Yang, Bai, Chen, & Rong, 2025).

ROT opens RBV's black box by specifying what managers actually do with resources.

### 2.2 The three orchestration processes

Sirmon et al. (2007) define resource management as three sequential managerial processes:

| ROT process | Definition | Managerial question |
|---|---|---|
| **Structuring** | Acquiring, accumulating, and divesting resources to form the resource portfolio | *Which* FinTech resources does the firm select, retain, or discard? |
| **Bundling** | Combining resources into capabilities — stabilising, enriching, or pioneering | *How* are payments, credit, compliance, and trust resources integrated into operating routines? |
| **Leveraging** | Mobilising, coordinating, and deploying capabilities in the marketplace | *Where* and *when* is the integrated stack deployed for specific foreign-market entries? |

Sirmon et al. (2011) integrate this resource-management framework with asset-orchestration research (Teece, 2007) into **Resource Orchestration Theory**, demonstrating that orchestration requirements vary systematically with firm scope, life-cycle stage, and managerial level. That contextual architecture matters directly for a cross-country, cross-firm qualitative design examining organisational and institutional contingencies.

### 2.3 Why "orchestration" and not "management" or "adoption"

**Adoption models** (including technology-acceptance frameworks) explain intention to acquire a technology. They stop at the firm's boundary. **Performance-correlates models** treat FinTech as an independent variable. Neither specifies the **intra-firm process** through which multiple FinTech services become integrated cross-border capability.

**Orchestration** is the precise term because FinTech value arises from **configuration of complementary resources**, not from any single tool. A payment gateway without compliant onboarding, or credit without transaction history, or compliance without interoperable data flows, does not produce internationalisation capability. ROT's bundling logic captures this complementarity; adoption counts do not.

## 3. Why ROT is the appropriate lens for FinTech specifically

### 3.1 The rarity condition fails at the resource level

FinTech resources — payment gateways, digital wallets, compliance automation, API-based data services — are predominantly **externally supplied, subscription- or transaction-priced services**. Any firm in the ecosystem can adopt the same provider tomorrow.

Under Barney's (1991) logic:

- **Valuable?** Often yes — FinTech reduces payment friction, credit gaps, and compliance costs.
- **Rare?** No — at the individual-resource level.
- **Inimitable?** No — competitors can subscribe to the same services.
- **Non-substitutable?** Weakly — alternative providers typically exist.

No individual FinTech resource can be a source of sustained advantage. Whatever advantage exists — if it exists — must reside in **firm-specific configuration**: which resources are selected, how they are integrated, and how the integrated stack is deployed across foreign markets. That is precisely the domain ROT theorises.

The FinTech context is therefore not merely a setting where orchestration *might* matter. It is a setting where orchestration is the **only candidate mechanism** for resource-based advantage.

### 3.2 Complementarity demands bundling

The study classifies FinTech resources functionally into four interdependent classes (Section 2.4 of Chapter 2):

1. Transaction and settlement  
2. Financing and liquidity  
3. Identity, compliance, and risk  
4. Trust, assurance, and data-infrastructure  

These classes are **functionally interdependent**. Settling a foreign payment requires compliant onboarding (classes 1 and 3). Trade credit requires transaction histories as scoring inputs (classes 2 and 1). Trust with unknown foreign partners requires verifiable records (class 4) derived from payment and compliance systems (classes 1 and 3).

Value arises from **interactions among classes**, not from any class in isolation — the resource-level expression of ROT's bundling logic. A firm with two tightly integrated, strategically fitted resources may be better endowed, in the theoretically relevant sense, than a firm with six disconnected ones. Adoption measures therefore **mismeasure** the construct that matters — which predicts the anomalies the empirical literature cannot absorb (Section 4 below).

### 3.3 Observable empirical referents

ROT's abstractions map onto **observable managerial decisions** — a requirement for qualitative case research:

| ROT process | FinTech decision examples (episode-level) |
|---|---|
| Structuring | Selecting a cross-border payment provider; adopting vs. divesting a digital credit platform; choosing RegTech vendor |
| Bundling | Integrating payment confirmation into order-to-cash workflow; linking KYC data to customer onboarding pipeline; connecting transaction history to credit scoring |
| Leveraging | Deploying integrated stack for Ghana entry vs. UK entry; reconfiguring compliance module for new licensing regime; coordinating FinTech stack with foreign distributor onboarding |

This mapping disciplines data collection (Chapter 3 interview protocol), coding (template analysis codes), and cross-case comparison. A theory without observable referents would be unfalsifiable in case research. ROT passes that test.

## 4. Empirical warrant: anomalies ROT explains and direct-effect models cannot

Three findings within the FinTech–digitalisation literature triangulate on ROT's central claim: **what the firm makes of technology matters more than whether it has technology**.

**Anomaly 1 — null direct effect among internationalised firms.** Digitalisation showed a significant direct effect on performance for domestic Finnish SMEs but **no effect** for internationalised SMEs (Joensuu-Salo, Sorama, Viljamaa, & Varamäki, 2018). Later work found that digital **capability** — a firm-level competence, not an adoption level — predicts performance and growth (Joensuu-Salo & Matalamäki, 2023). For firms already operating internationally, having technology explains little; what the firm has **made of** the technology explains more. That is an orchestration result reported in adoption vocabulary.

**Anomaly 2 — technology as double-edged input.** Digital platforms generate risks that **reduce** international new ventures' internationalisation scope; the negative effect is mitigated by entrepreneurial orientation (Jean, Kim, & Cavusgil, 2020). A resource whose net effect depends on firm-level strategic posture is not a direct-effect variable; it is an input into a firm-level process.

**Anomaly 3 — heterogeneity among similar adopters.** Firms in the same ecosystems with access to the same FinTech services diverge sharply in international outcomes. Under direct-effect models this variance is error. Under ROT it is **theoretical signal** — evidence of differential structuring, bundling, and leveraging.

ROT is therefore not imposed on a literature that already explains the phenomenon. It is the **theoretical response** to a pattern of findings the literature's own analytical structure cannot accommodate.

## 5. FTRO: extension of ROT, not a new theory

### 5.1 Definition and scope

**FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO)** is defined as:

> *The patterned managerial process through which an SME acquires, configures, integrates, deploys, and reconfigures complementary FinTech resources to support cross-border activities.*

FTRO is novel in **domain** (externally sourced digital financial services), **purpose** (cross-border expansion), and **setting** (institutionally thin emerging markets). It is **not** novel in process categories, which are drawn faithfully from ROT:

```text
ROT (general)                    FTRO (this study)
─────────────                    ─────────────────
Structuring portfolio      →     Selecting/divesting FinTech providers and services
Bundling into capabilities →     Integrating payment, credit, compliance, trust resources
Leveraging in market       →     Deploying integrated stack across foreign-market episodes
```

### 5.2 What the study claims — and what it does not

**Claims:**

- ROT provides the mechanism through which FinTech resources become cross-border capabilities.
- FTRO extends ROT into a domain ROT has not previously addressed at firm level.
- Orchestration — not adoption — is the theoretically decisive variable in FinTech-enabled internationalisation.

**Does not claim:**

- That FTRO is automatically a dynamic capability (treated as empirical question).
- That FTRO replaces INV, institutional theory, or network theory.
- That structuring, bundling, and leveraging are FinTech-specific labels for generic "digital transformation."
- That every firm that adopts FinTech orchestrates (one-off adoption without integration is evidence *against* strong FTRO).

## 6. ROT in relation to adjacent theories

### 6.1 Dynamic capabilities (Teece, 2007)

Dynamic capabilities explain how firms sense opportunities, seize them, and **reconfigure** asset bases under changing conditions. ROT integrates with this stream (Sirmon et al., 2011) but operates at a **lower evidentiary threshold**:

| Criterion | Dynamic capability | FTRO (minimum) | FTRO (strong form) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evidence required | Repeated reconfiguration across environmental shifts | Patterned structuring, bundling, leveraging for cross-border activity | Demonstrable reconfiguration of FinTech stack across successive market entries |
| Claim in this study | Not assumed | Default expectation | Empirical finding if data support it |

A firm that once assembled an effective FinTech stack has orchestrated. Only a firm that demonstrably **reconfigures** that stack as markets and regulations shift possesses something approaching a dynamic capability. The study respects this bar and does not conflate FTRO with dynamic capabilities by definition.

### 6.2 Digital capability (Joensuu-Salo & Matalamäki, 2023)

Digital capability is a **firm-level competence outcome** — what the firm can do with digital technology. FTRO is a **process description** — how that competence is built in the FinTech domain. The relationship is sequential:

```text
FTRO (process)  →  Digital / FinTech-related capabilities (outcome)  →  Internationalisation outcomes
```

Digital capability appears in the framework as an **organisational readiness condition** (P5), not as a substitute for FTRO. Confusing process with outcome would collapse the framework's Stage 2 into Stage 3 and eliminate the mechanism the study proposes.

### 6.3 Technology acceptance (UTAUT and related models)

Technology-acceptance frameworks explain **adoption intention** — why firms acquire technologies. They are silent on **post-adoption configuration** and international outcomes. In this study, acceptance logic is **subsumed under structuring**: the decision to adopt a payment gateway or RegTech tool is a structuring act. What happens after adoption — integration, deployment, reconfiguration — is bundling and leveraging, which acceptance models do not theorise.

### 6.4 Digital affordances and platform literature (Autio et al., 2018; Li et al., 2025)

Digital-affordance and platform-firm research operates primarily at **ecosystem level** — how digital infrastructure enables entrepreneurial opportunity. Li et al. (2025) systematise digital platform-based international new ventures as **producers** of infrastructure. This study theorises **user SMEs** that configure externally sourced financial services. ROT supplies the firm-level mechanism that ecosystem-level affordance arguments presuppose but do not specify.

### 6.5 Direct-effect FinTech models

The dominant empirical pattern models FinTech as an antecedent with direct effects on access, efficiency, or internationalisation (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021; Hajizamani et al., 2025). These studies establish association; they do not specify mechanism. ROT is the **alternative analytical structure** this study proposes — not a rejection of their findings but an explanation of the heterogeneity and null effects those findings imply.

## 7. How ROT combines with INV in the FOI Framework

ROT and INV perform **non-overlapping theoretical work**:

```text
INV theory                          ROT / FTRO                         FOI Framework
──────────                          ──────────                         ─────────────
Names the internationalisation      Names the conversion mechanism     Integrates both:
pattern: early/rapid engagement,    through which FinTech resources    Portfolio → FTRO →
resource mobilisation, networks     become cross-border capabilities   Capabilities → Outcomes
```

| FOI stage | ROT role | INV role |
|---|---|---|
| Stage 1 — FinTech resource portfolio | Structuring defines portfolio composition | Resources accessed for cross-border mobilisation |
| Stage 2 — FTRO | **Core ROT contribution** | Enables INV-type resource access |
| Stage 3 — Capabilities | Output of bundling | Capabilities INV presumes but does not specify |
| Stage 4 — Outcomes | Output of leveraging | Speed, scope, intensity, persistence (INV pattern) |

Without ROT, the framework has resources and outcomes but no mechanism. Without INV, the framework has a conversion process but no disciplined internationalisation architecture. Together they form the FOI Framework.

## 8. Methodological implications of ROT grounding

ROT grounding is not only a theoretical choice; it **disciplines the entire research design**.

### 8.1 Unit of analysis: foreign-market-entry episode

Leveraging is **market-specific deployment**. The embedded unit of analysis is therefore the **foreign-market-entry episode** — the bounded sequence from market selection through FinTech configuration to first sustained foreign revenue (or stall/retrenchment). Episodes make structuring, bundling, and leveraging decisions reconstructable in retrospective interviews.

### 8.2 Interview protocol structure

Interview domains mirror ROT processes:

- **Structuring** — provider selection, adoption triggers, divestment, portfolio evolution  
- **Bundling** — integration decisions, workflow design, interoperability choices  
- **Leveraging** — market-specific deployment, reconfiguration, coordination with partners  

Neutral episode-based wording avoids leading informants toward adoption narratives and toward orchestration evidence.

### 8.3 Template analysis codes

Codebook thematic analysis (King, 2012) uses **a priori codes** derived from ROT (structuring, bundling, leveraging sub-codes) and the four capability constructs (outputs of bundling). Inductive extension captures practices that exceed the template; deviant cases (high adoption / low orchestration / weak outcomes) test P3's serial-mediation claim.

### 8.4 Cross-case comparison logic

Replication logic follows orchestration predictions:

- **Literal replication** — similar orchestration patterns → similar capability profiles → similar outcomes  
- **Theoretical replication** — weak orchestration → weak capabilities → stalled or retrenched internationalisation for predictable reasons  
- **Critical case** — high adoption with strong outcomes despite weak orchestration would challenge P3  

Without ROT, cross-case comparison would devolve into descriptive adoption inventories.

## 9. Proposition-level implications of ROT grounding

| Proposition | ROT grounding | Testable implication |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** Resource foundation | Structuring requires a portfolio to structure; endowment = fit, not count | Richer, more interoperable portfolios afford more orchestration choices |
| **P2** Capability formation | Bundling converts resources into capabilities | Structuring/bundling/leveraging evidence maps to four capability constructs |
| **P3** Serial mediation | ROT is the intervening process RBV/adoption models omit | High-adoption/low-orchestration firms show weak outcomes |
| **P4** Complementarity | Bundling logic implies super-additive returns to aligned configurations | Narrow-integrated portfolios outperform broad-disconnected ones |
| **P5** Organisational contingency | Structuring and bundling are absorptive, attention-dependent, costly | Readiness conditions portfolio→FTRO conversion |
| **P6** Institutional contingency | Leveraging is deployment-sensitive to institutional context | Capability→outcome conversion varies across Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa |

| Alternative mechanism | Why insufficient as primary grounding | Role in this study |

| **RBV (Barney, 1991)** | Managerial silence; rarity fails for FinTech resources | Motivating problem for ROT |

| **Grounded theory (induction only)** | Would ignore ROT foundation Chapter 2 provides | Rejected as primary logic; template analysis preferred |

ROT is the **primary mechanism anchor** because it alone specifies the structuring–bundling–leveraging process through which non-rare, complementary FinTech resources become cross-border capabilities — with observable referents, complementarity logic, and fit to the empirical anomalies the literature reports.

## 11. Anticipated objections and responses

**Objection 1: "You are merely relabelling digital capability or digital transformation as FTRO."**

**Response.** Digital capability is an outcome competence. Digital transformation is a change programme label without specified process categories. FTRO specifies **how** capability is built in the FinTech domain through three ROT-derived processes with direct empirical referents. If informants describe transformation rhetoric without integration evidence, that is data against strong FTRO — not confirmation of an existing construct under a new name.

**Objection 2: "Orchestration is a metaphor. Why not just say 'FinTech integration'?"**

**Response.** "Integration" captures bundling partially but omits structuring (portfolio selection) and leveraging (market-specific deployment). ROT's three-process architecture is **analytically distinct** and maps to sequential managerial decisions observed in internationalisation episodes. Integration alone would under-specify the mechanism and collapse the portfolio and deployment stages the framework requires.

**Objection 3: "FinTech resources are too commoditised for RBV or ROT to apply."**

**Response.** Commoditisation at the resource level is precisely why ROT applies. RBV fails at the individual-resource level; ROT was developed for contexts where advantage resides in **configuration**, not possession. Commoditised FinTech is the limiting case that makes orchestration the only candidate mechanism — not an objection to it.

**Objection 4: "ROT has never been applied to internationalisation or FinTech. Why import it?"**

**Response.** Theoretical import is justified when (a) the target phenomenon exhibits the conditions the theory was built to explain, and (b) existing theories in the target domain leave the mechanism unspecified. Both conditions hold. ROT's extension into FinTech-enabled internationalisation is the study's **contribution to ROT**, not an unexamined borrowing.

**Objection 5: "Why qualitative research if ROT supports hypothesis testing?"**

**Response.** The propositions are **working propositions** guiding pattern matching in cases (Yin, 2018), not parametric hypotheses for survey estimation. Qualitative research is appropriate for **opening the black box** — identifying orchestration practices, capability manifestations, and contingent conditions — that a later quantitative study could operationalise. The contribution is processual and conceptual refinement; P3's serial-mediation claim is illustrated and qualified in cases, not estimated with path coefficients.

**Objection 6: "Hajizamani et al. (2025) already propose an integrative FinTech–internationalisation framework."**

**Response.** Their review confirms FinTech as an enabler and proposes a multidimensional integrative framework at the **review level** — barriers, mediators, outcomes — but does not theorise the **intra-firm orchestration process** through which multiple FinTech resources become capabilities. The FOI Framework's novelty is FTRO as a mechanism, ROT-grounded process categories, four functional resource classes with complementarity logic, and six propositions linked to RQ1–RQ4. The relationship is **extension**, not duplication.

**Objection 7: "Managers do not think in terms of structuring, bundling, and leveraging."**

**Response.** Informants need not use ROT vocabulary. The interview protocol uses episode-based, practitioner language (provider choices, workflow integration, market deployment). Structuring, bundling, and leveraging are **analytical categories** applied in coding — standard practice in theoretically informed qualitative research. ROT's value is analytical precision, not managerial vernacular.

## 12. Contribution to Resource Orchestration Theory

The study contributes to ROT in three specified ways:

1. **New resource domain.** ROT's empirical applications have focused primarily on owned, internal resources. FinTech resources are externally sourced, subscription-priced, and non-rare — extending ROT into a domain where the rarity condition fails by construction and configuration is the only advantage candidate.

2. **New strategic purpose.** ROT literature examines value creation in product markets generally. This study applies orchestration to **cross-border expansion** — a purpose that imposes complementarity requirements (settlement + compliance + credit + trust) absent in domestic orchestration contexts.

3. **New contextual setting.** ROT's contextual claims (Sirmon et al., 2011) are tested in **institutionally thin emerging markets**, where FinTech operates as an institutional substitute and leveraging is deployment-sensitive to regulatory fragmentation, voids, and infrastructure reliability.

The contribution can be stated with precision:

> **This study extends Resource Orchestration Theory into externally sourced digital financial services deployed for cross-border expansion by emerging-market SMEs — specifying FTRO as the mechanism through which non-rare FinTech resources become internationalisation capabilities, and thereby explaining outcome heterogeneity that possession-based and direct-effect models treat as error variance.**

---

## 13. Conclusion: the precise claim

ROT is the correct mechanism grounding for this study because:

1. **Problem fit** — the capability-generation gap is a problem of managerial action (how resources become capabilities), which RBV alone cannot address and which direct-effect models leave unspecified.
2. **Resource fit** — FinTech resources fail RBV rarity at the individual level; advantage — if it exists — must reside in firm-specific configuration, which is ROT's domain.
3. **Complementarity fit** — FinTech's four functional resource classes are interdependent; value arises from bundling, not isolated adoption.
4. **Empirical fit** — null direct effects, double-edged technology findings, and adopters' heterogeneity are orchestration results reported in adoption vocabulary.
5. **Methodological fit** — structuring, bundling, and leveraging map onto observable episode-level decisions, disciplining interviews, coding, and cross-case comparison.
6. **Complementarity with INV** — ROT supplies the mechanism INV presumes; INV supplies the internationalisation outcomes FTRO is theorised to produce.

The study does not claim that FinTech adoption causes internationalisation. It claims that **FinTech Resource Orchestration** — structuring, bundling, and leveraging complementary FinTech resources — converts portfolio endowment into cross-border capabilities that drive, accelerate, or fail to sustain internationalisation outcomes. That is the ROT-grounded core of the FOI Framework.
