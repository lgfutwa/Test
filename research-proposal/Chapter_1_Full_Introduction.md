## 1.1 Introduction

This study investigates how African small and medium enterprises (SMEs) convert financial technology (FinTech) into the capabilities required for rapid and sustained internationalisation. Its central claim, developed theoretically in Chapter 2 and examined empirically through the design in Chapter 3, is that internationalisation outcomes depend not on the adoption of FinTech but on its *orchestration*: the managerial process through which firms structure, bundle, and leverage complementary FinTech resources into cross-border capabilities. The study names this process FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO) and embeds it in a four-stage process model — the FinTech-Orchestrated Internationalisation (FOI) Framework — that integrates resource orchestration theory (Sirmon, Hitt, & Ireland, 2007; Sirmon, Hitt, Ireland, & Gilbert, 2011) with international new venture (INV) theory (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994, 2005).

This chapter establishes why the study is needed and what it will deliver. Section 1.2 presents the business problem: the paradox of African SMEs operating amid world-leading FinTech ecosystems while remaining marginal in international trade. Section 1.3 presents the academic problem — deliberately separated from the business problem — and states the study's theoretical hook. Section 1.4 formulates the purpose statement, research questions, and objectives. Section 1.5 defines the scope and boundary conditions. Section 1.6 sets out the intended theoretical, practical, and policy contributions. Section 1.7 defines key terms, and Section 1.8 outlines the structure of the remainder of the document.

## 1.2 Background to the Research Problem: The Business Rationale

### 1.2.1 African SMEs and the internationalisation imperative

Small and medium enterprises carry a disproportionate share of Africa's economic activity and employment, and their growth trajectories matter accordingly for development outcomes across the continent. For a growing class of African SMEs — particularly technology-enabled service firms in software, digital services, e-commerce operations, and business-process outsourcing — domestic markets alone cannot sustain growth: addressable local demand is thin relative to fixed costs, revenue is concentrated in volatile local currencies, and the marginal cost of serving a foreign customer digitally is low. For these firms internationalisation is not an aspiration but an operating necessity. Yet cross-border activity confronts them with a compounding set of frictions: converting and settling payments across currencies, financing receivables and working capital without collateral, satisfying foreign know-your-customer (KYC) and anti-money-laundering (AML) regimes, and establishing trust with counterparties who have no way to verify an unknown African firm's bona fides.

These frictions are anchored in well-documented structural conditions. Financing constraints bind small firms disproportionately: smaller firms report more severe financing obstacles than large firms, and those obstacles have a greater dampening effect on their growth (Beck & Demirgüç-Kunt, 2006). The formal financing gap for micro, small, and medium enterprises in Sub-Saharan Africa is estimated at approximately USD 331 billion (International Finance Corporation, 2017). Beyond finance, the institutional context raises the cost of every cross-border transaction: where market-supporting intermediaries — credit bureaus, enforcement mechanisms, certification bodies — are weak or absent, transaction costs rise and impersonal exchange contracts (Khanna & Palepu, 1997; North, 1990). The foreign firm's classic disadvantage, the liability of foreignness (Zaheer, 1995), is compounded outbound for African SMEs because thin home-country institutions cannot certify their credibility to foreign counterparties.

### 1.2.2 The FinTech transformation

Against this backdrop, Sub-Saharan Africa has become one of the world's most significant sites of financial-technology innovation — driven, in substantial part, precisely by the institutional gaps described above, since FinTech develops fastest where incumbent financial institutions leave needs unserved (Haddad & Hornuf, 2019). Mobile money has achieved mass adoption with documented welfare effects: access to Kenya's M-Pesa network increased household consumption and lifted an estimated two percent of Kenyan households out of extreme poverty (Suri & Jack, 2016), and mobile-money accounts and transaction values across the region continue to grow rapidly (GSMA, 2024). Digital lending platforms extend credit using alternative data where collateral-based bank lending is inaccessible, with evidence that peer-to-peer lending FinTechs materially increase SME access to finance (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, Brohi, & Nasim, 2021). Regulatory technology (RegTech) automates the compliance functions — onboarding, screening, reporting — whose fixed costs otherwise price small firms out of regulated cross-border activity (Arner, Barberis, & Buckley, 2017). Data-infrastructure and assurance technologies, from interoperable APIs to distributed ledgers, substitute verifiable records for the interpersonal and institutional trust that cross-border exchange normally presupposes (Gomber, Kauffman, Parker, & Weber, 2018).

Each of the frictions in Section 1.2.1 therefore now has a candidate technological remedy, available to African SMEs as externally supplied, subscription- or transaction-priced services.

### 1.2.3 The paradox of plenty

The business problem is that the remedy has not produced the cure at any predictable rate. African SMEs now operate amid an unusually rich FinTech ecosystem, yet many still fail to internationalise, or internationalise and then retrench — and, most consequentially for managers and policymakers, firms with access to the *same* FinTech ecosystem achieve starkly different international outcomes. For the SME founder allocating scarce funds among a payment gateway, a credit facility, and a compliance tool ahead of a first foreign market entry, the practically urgent question is no longer *whether* FinTech matters — the evidence that it does is extensive — but *which configurations of FinTech convert into foreign revenue, and why apparently similar investments so often do not*. For policymakers designing digital-trade and financial-inclusion policy, the corresponding question is which institutional conditions allow SME FinTech investments to pay off across borders. Neither question can currently be answered with confidence, because — as Section 1.3 argues — the research base has not been designed to answer them.

## 1.3 The Academic Problem and Theoretical Hook

### 1.3.1 The academic problem

The academic problem is distinct from the business problem and must be stated in its own terms. Existing research predominantly examines the *association* between FinTech adoption (or digital-technology adoption) and internationalisation or performance outcomes, treating FinTech as an enabling antecedent while leaving the organisational mechanism through which FinTech resources are transformed into internationalisation capabilities largely unspecified. Three literatures converge on this omission without resolving it; Chapter 2 develops each argument in full, and they are summarised here to establish the problem.

First, **INV theory explains why early and rapid internationalisation is possible but presumes its financial resource base.** The theory holds that ventures can be international from inception by internalising some transactions, relying on alternative governance structures to access resources, establishing foreign location advantages, and controlling unique resources (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994), with internationalisation speed modelled as an outcome enabled by technology and moderated by knowledge and networks (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005; Coviello, 2006; Knight & Cavusgil, 2004). But "accessing resources through alternative governance structures" presupposes that the venture can transact across borders at all — receive foreign payments, finance working capital, satisfy foreign compliance, establish trust with unknown counterparties. In the developed-market settings where the theory was formulated, that financial infrastructure was ambient; in Sub-Saharan Africa it is not, and the theory is silent on how resource-constrained firms assemble it.

Second, **the resource-based tradition contains the needed explanatory machinery, but it has not been extended to FinTech.** Resources alone do not confer advantage (Barney, 1991); resource orchestration theory specifies the managerial actions — structuring, bundling, and leveraging — through which resource endowments become value-creating capabilities (Sirmon et al., 2007, 2011). No prior study, to the researcher's knowledge, has applied this orchestration logic to the portfolio of financial technologies available to internationalising SMEs.

Third, **the FinTech and digital-internationalisation literatures document enabling effects but not capability formation** — and contain findings their own direct-effect designs cannot absorb. Digitalisation shows no direct performance effect among already-internationalised SMEs (Joensuu-Salo, Sorama, Viljamaa, & Varamäki, 2018); digital platforms generate risks that reduce, rather than expand, the internationalisation scope of international new ventures (Jean, Kim, & Cavusgil, 2020); and recent systematic reviews conclude that finance, technology, and networks operate as complements whose integration existing theory does not explain (Anwar, Li, Al-Omush, & Al-Nimer, 2023; Bargoni, Ferraris, Vilamová, & Wan Hussain, 2024; Hajizamani, Zakery, & Bonyadi Naieni, 2025). These anomalies point to a missing intervening term: the organisational process between resource and outcome. Chapter 2 (Section 2.5) formalises this as the **capability-generation gap**.

### 1.3.2 The theoretical hook

The study's theoretical hook is the specification of that missing term. **FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO)** is defined as *the patterned managerial process through which an SME acquires, configures, integrates, deploys, and reconfigures complementary FinTech resources to support cross-border activities*. The FOI Framework proposes that FinTech resources — classified functionally into transaction and settlement, financing and liquidity, identity and compliance, and trust and data-infrastructure resources — are converted through FTRO into four cross-border internationalisation capabilities (financial-operational, regulatory-adaptation, network-mobilisation, and opportunity-enactment), which in turn drive internationalisation outcomes (entry speed, scope, intensity, and persistence). Organisational readiness conditions the resource-to-orchestration conversion; institutional conditions condition the capability-to-outcome conversion.

The hook has a sharp theoretical warrant: because FinTech resources are externally supplied services available to any firm, no individual FinTech resource can satisfy the rarity condition for resource-based advantage (Barney, 1991) — so whatever advantage exists must reside in firm-specific configuration, which is precisely what orchestration theorises. The FinTech context is thus not merely a setting where orchestration might matter; it is a setting where orchestration is the only candidate mechanism for resource-based advantage. In shifting the explanatory focus from *whether* FinTech enables internationalisation to *how and under what conditions* FinTech resources become internationalisation capabilities, the study converts the literature's unexplained outcome heterogeneity from error variance into theoretical signal.

## 1.4 Purpose Statement, Research Questions, and Objectives

### 1.4.1 Purpose statement

The purpose of this qualitative multiple-case study is to explain how rapidly internationalising, technology-enabled service SMEs in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa orchestrate FinTech resources into internationalisation capabilities, and how organisational and institutional conditions shape that process.

### 1.4.2 Research questions

**Primary research question:**

> How do African technology-enabled service SMEs orchestrate FinTech resources to develop the capabilities required for rapid and sustained internationalisation?

**Sub-questions:**

- **RQ1 (Orchestration processes):** How do these SMEs structure, bundle, and leverage FinTech resources — across transaction and settlement, financing and liquidity, identity and compliance, and trust and data-infrastructure functions — in the course of internationalising?
- **RQ2 (Capability formation):** What cross-border internationalisation capabilities are generated through these orchestration processes?
- **RQ3 (Outcomes):** How do these capabilities influence the speed, scope, intensity, and persistence of the SMEs' internationalisation?
- **RQ4 (Contextual contingencies):** How do organisational readiness (absorptive capacity, digital capability, leadership commitment, financial slack) and institutional conditions (regulatory fragmentation, institutional voids, digital infrastructure, cybersecurity) shape FTRO and its outcomes?

The questions map one-to-one onto the FOI Framework: RQ1 interrogates the orchestration stage (propositions P1 and P4), RQ2 the capability stage (P2), RQ3 the capability–outcome link (P3), and RQ4 the two contingencies (P5 and P6). This mapping — maintained through the consistency matrix, the interview protocol, and the analysis template — constitutes the study's golden thread.

### 1.4.3 Research objectives

1. Reconstruct, within each case, the sequence of FinTech structuring, bundling, and leveraging decisions taken during internationalisation.
2. Identify and characterise the cross-border capabilities these orchestration processes generate.
3. Trace how those capabilities relate to observed internationalisation outcomes across each firm's foreign-market-entry episodes.
4. Identify the organisational and institutional conditions that enable or constrain FTRO, and refine the FOI Framework's six propositions accordingly.

## 1.5 Scope and Boundary Conditions

The study is deliberately bounded, and its claims are disciplined by those bounds.

- **Population.** Technology-enabled service SMEs (10–250 employees, operational for at least three years) headquartered in Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa, that have undertaken international activity and use at least one FinTech resource in cross-border operations. Claims are made about this population — not about African SMEs in general — with analytical generalisation to theory (Yin, 2018).
- **Geographic context.** Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa are selected on theoretical grounds: they host Africa's three most developed FinTech ecosystems — so the resource portfolio is richly present in all three — while differing sharply in regulatory architecture, payment-system structure, and institutional depth, supplying the cross-context variation RQ4 requires. The context is of theoretical import: where institutions are thin, FinTech operates as an institutional substitute, rendering the orchestration mechanism observable in sharper relief than in developed markets.
- **Internationalisation pathways.** The study distinguishes rapid internationalisers (first foreign revenue within three years of founding, consistent with born-global operationalisations; Knight & Cavusgil, 2004) from later internationalisers, and deliberately includes both — as well as firms whose internationalisation stalled or retrenched — to avoid sampling on success and to expose the framework to disconfirmation.
- **Phenomenon.** The study examines the orchestration of FinTech resources *by user SMEs*; it does not study FinTech firms themselves as producers, nor consumer financial inclusion, except as context.
- **Design horizon.** The study is cross-sectional with retrospective process reconstruction (Chapter 3); longitudinal observation of orchestration is identified as future research.

## 1.6 Significance and Intended Contribution

### 1.6.1 Theoretical contribution

The study makes three theoretical contributions, argued in full in Chapter 2 (Section 2.7.3). **To FinTech research**, it reconceptualises FinTech from an adoption variable into a configurable portfolio of four functional resource classes whose value depends on purposeful orchestration — supplying the firm-level theory of resource *use* that a literature of ecosystems, fields, and determinants lacks (Lee & Shin, 2018; Gomber, Koch, & Siering, 2017; Haddad & Hornuf, 2019). **To resource orchestration theory**, it extends structuring–bundling–leveraging (Sirmon et al., 2011) into a new domain defined by externally sourced, non-rare digital resources deployed for cross-border purposes in institutionally thin settings — while treating the dynamic-capability status of FTRO as an empirical question rather than a definitional assumption (Teece, 2007). **To international entrepreneurship**, it supplies the capability-generation mechanism that INV theory presumes but does not specify (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994; Cavusgil & Knight, 2015; Zahra, 2021), explaining heterogeneous internationalisation outcomes among firms with similar technology access.

### 1.6.2 Practical and policy significance

For **SME managers**, the study aims to replace tool-level advice ("adopt FinTech") with configuration-level guidance: which combinations of transaction, financing, compliance, and trust resources support entry into and persistence in foreign markets, in what sequence, and under which internal conditions the investment pays off. For **FinTech providers**, evidence on how SMEs bundle services across providers identifies interoperability and integration as competitive parameters, informing product and partnership strategy. For **policymakers and regulators**, the study speaks to the conditions — payment interoperability, regulatory coherence, licensing design, infrastructure reliability — under which SME FinTech investment converts into export capability, informing digital-trade and financial-sector policy at national level and within continental integration initiatives.

## 1.7 Definition of Key Terms

- **FinTech (financial technology):** digitally delivered financial services and infrastructures — spanning payments, credit, compliance, and data/assurance functions — supplied to firms predominantly as externally sourced services (Gomber et al., 2017; Lee & Shin, 2018).
- **FinTech resource portfolio:** the set of FinTech resources available to and used by a firm, classified functionally into transaction and settlement; financing and liquidity; identity, compliance, and risk; and trust, assurance, and data-infrastructure resources; endowment is assessed on availability, quality, interoperability, accessibility, and strategic fit.
- **FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO):** the patterned managerial process through which an SME acquires, configures, integrates, deploys, and reconfigures complementary FinTech resources to support cross-border activities; comprising structuring, bundling, and leveraging (after Sirmon et al., 2011).
- **Cross-border internationalisation capabilities:** the four firm-level capabilities theorised to result from FTRO — financial-operational, regulatory-adaptation, network-mobilisation, and opportunity-enactment.
- **Internationalisation outcomes:** entry speed (time from internationalisation decision to first foreign transaction), international scope, international intensity, and post-entry persistence and growth.
- **International new venture (INV):** a business organisation that, from inception, seeks significant competitive advantage from the use of resources and the sale of outputs in multiple countries (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994); operationalised in this study via first foreign revenue within three years of founding (Knight & Cavusgil, 2004).
- **Liability of foreignness / outsidership:** the systematic costs a firm bears in foreign markets relative to local competitors (Zaheer, 1995), and the disadvantage of exclusion from relevant business networks (Johanson & Vahlne, 2009).
- **Institutional voids:** the absence or weakness of market-supporting intermediaries that facilitate exchange in developed markets (Khanna & Palepu, 1997).
- **SME:** for this study, a firm employing 10–250 people, operational for at least three years.

## 1.8 Structure of the Document

The remainder of the document proceeds as follows. **Chapter 2** develops the theoretical foundation: it reviews the SME internationalisation, resource orchestration, and FinTech literatures as a single cumulative argument; specifies the capability-generation gap through three anomalies in the empirical record; theorises the organisational and institutional contingencies of African markets, including the non-monotonic role of institutional voids; and integrates the argument into the FOI Framework with six propositions (P1–P6). **Chapter 3** presents and defends the research methodology: an interpretivist, abductive, qualitative multiple-case design across Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa, with purposive maximum-variation sampling including stalled internationalisers, episode-based semi-structured interviews, codebook thematic analysis with cross-case pattern matching, an operationalised thematic-sufficiency rule, and a full trustworthiness and ethics architecture. **Chapter 4** reports the findings by research question, using within-case narratives, a first-order/second-order data structure, and cross-case matrices. **Chapter 5** discusses the findings against the literature of Chapter 2, refines the FOI Framework and its propositions, and develops implications for theory, management, and policy. **Chapter 6** concludes with the study's contribution, limitations, and an agenda for future research.

## 1.9 Conclusion

This chapter has framed the study around two rigorously separated problems. The business problem is a paradox of plenty: African SMEs operate amid world-leading FinTech ecosystems yet internationalise unevenly, and managers lack configuration-level guidance about which FinTech investments convert into foreign revenue. The academic problem is a capability-generation gap: the literature models FinTech as an enabling antecedent with direct effects, leaving unspecified the organisational mechanism through which financial technologies become internationalisation capabilities — a mechanism that INV theory presumes, that resource orchestration theory can supply, and that no prior study has specified for the FinTech domain. The study's response — the FOI Framework, with FTRO as its core mechanism — yields four research questions and six propositions whose theoretical development (Chapter 2) and empirical examination (Chapter 3) the following chapters undertake.
