---

## 1. Thesis of the argument

This study is grounded in **International New Venture (INV) theory** because the phenomenon under investigation is not gradual, experiential internationalisation but **early, rapid, or attempted-rapid cross-border engagement by resource-constrained technology-enabled service SMEs** — the population and pattern INV theory was designed to explain. INV supplies the **internationalisation outcome architecture** (what counts as internationalisation, which mechanisms matter, and what must be explained). It does **not** supply the **capability-generation mechanism** for FinTech resources; that mechanism is theorised through Resource Orchestration Theory (ROT) as FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO). The FOI Framework therefore **extends** INV theory rather than replacing it: INV names the pattern; FTRO explains how the financial preconditions of that pattern are assembled in institutionally thin emerging markets.

---

## 2. What INV theory explains — and why that matches this study

### 2.1 The phenomenon INV was built for

The Uppsala tradition treats internationalisation as incremental commitment driven by experiential learning (Johanson & Vahlne, 1977). Its later revisions rightly emphasise networks and the liability of outsidership (Johanson & Vahlne, 2009) and dynamic capabilities (Vahlne & Johanson, 2017). Yet even in evolved form, the incremental account is calibrated to firms that can afford to learn slowly — entering psychically close markets first, deepening commitment stepwise, and treating uncertainty as something reduced over time through experience.

INV theory arose to explain a different population: **ventures that are international at or near founding** (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994). Its core insight is that internationalisation does not require accumulated scale or long domestic tenure. What matters is whether the venture can:

1. **Internalise** some transactions rather than relying entirely on market exchange;
2. **Access resources through alternative governance structures** — partnerships, licensing, networks — when it lacks ownership;
3. **Establish foreign location advantages** that justify cross-border presence; and
4. **Control unique resources** that sustain differentiation abroad.

This is precisely the strategic posture of the technology-enabled service SMEs in this study. They are young relative to large incumbents, rarely possess the balance sheets of established exporters, and depend on networks, platforms, and external service providers to reach foreign customers. Their internationalisation logic is mobilisation and configuration, not slow experiential deepening.

### 2.2 Born-global refinement: capabilities, not asset stocks

Born-global research consolidated the INV insight empirically: early internationalisers succeed through **intangible advantages and organisational capabilities** — innovation culture, entrepreneurial orientation, and specific competences — rather than through large tangible resource stocks (Knight & Cavusgil, 2004; Cavusgil & Knight, 2015). Knight and Liesch (2016) further established that early internationalisation is now a general phenomenon, not a high-technology curiosity.

This matters for two reasons in the present study.

**First**, it legitimises studying **rapid and attempted-rapid internationalisers** as theoretically central cases, not outliers to be explained away by incremental models.

**Second**, it shifts explanatory attention from *what the firm owns* to *what the firm can do* — which aligns with the study's capability-stage constructs (financial-operational, regulatory-adaptation, network-mobilisation, opportunity-enactment) rather than with adoption counts or asset inventories.

### 2.3 Network dynamics as constitutive, not decorative

Coviello (2006) showed that network relationships are not incidental to INV internationalisation; they are **constitutive** of it. Networks supply market knowledge, credibility, and access that young ventures cannot generate internally. For African technology-enabled service SMEs operating from institutionally thin home markets, foreign network ties are often the primary substitute for the certifications, credit histories, and reputational signals that developed-market firms take for granted.

INV theory therefore supplies the **network-mobilisation logic** that the FOI Framework embeds in Stage 3 capabilities — not as a separate add-on but as an element INV already recognises as central to early internationalisation.

### 2.4 Speed as outcome, not capability

Oviatt and McDougall (2005) modelled internationalisation **speed as an outcome** — enabled by technology, mediated by entrepreneurial opportunity perception, and moderated by knowledge and network relationships — not as a capability firms possess. This construct discipline is non-negotiable for the present study:

| Term | Status in this study | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Entry speed | **Outcome** (Stage 4) | Dependent variable to be explained |
| International scope, intensity, persistence | **Outcomes** (Stage 4) | Observable internationalisation results |
| Financial-operational capability | **Capability** (Stage 3) | What the firm can do with FinTech across borders |
| Regulatory-adaptation capability | **Capability** (Stage 3) | What the firm can do under foreign compliance regimes |
| Network-mobilisation capability | **Capability** (Stage 3) | What the firm can do through foreign ties |
| Opportunity-enactment capability | **Capability** (Stage 3) | What the firm can do to operationalise foreign opportunities |

**"Resource leverage," "speed-to-market," and "network coordination" are not treated as canonical INV constructs.** They are informal descriptors sometimes used in practitioner discourse. The study's constructs are defined in ROT-informed capability terms and mapped to INV's resource, governance, and network mechanisms — not relabelled as if they were established INV variables.

## 3. Why INV — and not the incremental tradition alone

The incremental and INV traditions are not mutually exclusive at the level of history; both converge on capabilities and networks as explanatory cores (Vahlne & Johanson, 2017; Coviello, 2006). The choice between them is a **phenomenon fit** choice.

| Criterion | Incremental / Uppsala tradition | INV / born-global tradition | Fit for this study |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary population | Firms with time to learn and deepen commitment | Young, resource-constrained ventures internationalising early or rapidly | **INV** — sampled SMEs are technology-enabled service firms whose competitive logic is early foreign reach |
| Principal obstacle modelled | Uncertainty reduced through experience | Resource mobilisation through alternative governance and networks | **INV** — principal obstacles are financial-infrastructural (payment, credit, compliance, trust), not slow psychic-distance learning |
| Speed of internationalisation | Emerges from commitment deepening | Legitimate object of explanation from inception | **INV** — study includes rapid, later, and **stalled** internationalisers to avoid success-only sampling |
| Network role | Evolved into liability of outsidership | Constitutive from founding | **Both**, but INV foregrounds network-dependent mobilisation |
| Technology role | Secondary in classic formulations | Explicit enabler of speed (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005) | **INV** — FinTech is the technology class under investigation |

The study does **not** reject incremental models wholesale. Later internationalisers in the sample can be read through an accelerated-commitment lens. But the **theoretical anchor** is INV because the research question concerns how firms develop the capabilities required for **rapid and sustained** internationalisation — the pattern INV theory exists to explain.

## 4. The gap INV leaves open — and why that gap justifies this study

### 4.1 The undisclosed presumption

INV theory's second necessary element — "strong reliance on alternative governance structures to access resources" (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994) — presupposes that the venture can **transact across borders at all**:

- receive payment from foreign customers;
- finance receivables and working capital;
- satisfy foreign KYC, AML, and licensing requirements; and
- establish sufficient trust with unknown foreign counterparties.

In the developed-market settings where INV theory was formulated, this **financial infrastructure was ambient**. Banks, credit bureaus, payment rails, and enforcement institutions were taken for granted. The theory therefore explains *that* resource-constrained ventures can internationalise early; it does not explain *how* they assemble the financial preconditions of cross-border exchange when those preconditions are absent.

In Sub-Saharan Africa, that presumption fails:

- financing constraints bind SMEs disproportionately (Beck & Demirgüç-Kunt, 2006);
- the formal MSME financing gap is large (International Finance Corporation, 2017);
- institutional voids raise transaction costs (Khanna & Palepu, 1997; North, 1990); and
- the liability of foreignness (Zaheer, 1995) is compounded outbound by thin home-country institutions that would otherwise certify the firm's bona fides.

FinTech operates as an **institutional substitute** in such settings (Suri & Jack, 2016; Haddad & Hornuf, 2019). The theoretically sharp question is therefore not whether technology matters — INV already admits technology as an enabler of speed — but **how resource-constrained SMEs configure substitutes into operating capability**.

That question is INV's open gap. The FOI Framework answers it.

### 4.2 Why the digital entrepreneurship turn has not closed the gap

The digital turn in international entrepreneurship has advanced the field substantially. Nambisan (2017) theorised how digital technology transforms entrepreneurial opportunity; Autio et al. (2018) placed digital affordances at the centre of ecosystem dynamics; Nambisan, Wright, and Feldman (2019) mapped digitalisation as a general research programme; and Zahra (2021) argued that post-COVID conditions tighten the coupling between digital capability and international venturing.

Yet this literature engages digital technology **generically** — platforms, connectivity, affordances — and has not theorised the specifically **financial** technologies that constitute the transactional preconditions of cross-border exchange. Jean, Kim, and Cavusgil (2020) showed that digital platforms can **reduce** INV internationalisation scope under certain conditions — a finding that confirms technology is not a direct-effect variable but an input into firm-level process. Yang et al. (2025) and Li et al. (2025) consolidated digital-firm and platform-firm literatures respectively and independently called for integrative frameworks; Hajizamani et al. (2025) reviewed FinTech and SME internationalisation specifically but stopped at the enabler level.

**None of these streams specifies FTRO** — the firm-level process through which FinTech resources become cross-border capabilities. INV grounding is therefore not redundant with digital entrepreneurship; it is the **internationalisation anchor** that digital entrepreneurship has not yet connected to FinTech-specific resource configuration.

## 5. How INV and ROT combine in the FOI Framework

The study's theoretical architecture is a **deliberate division of labour**, not a loose eclecticism.

```text
INV theory                          ROT / FTRO                         FOI Framework
──────────                          ──────────                         ─────────────
Names the internationalisation      Names the conversion mechanism     Integrates both:
pattern to be explained:            through which FinTech resources    Portfolio → FTRO →
early/rapid foreign engagement      become cross-border capabilities   Capabilities → Outcomes
through resource mobilisation,      (structuring, bundling,            (with contingencies)
alternative governance, and         leveraging complementary
network coordination                FinTech resource classes)
```

### 5.1 Mapping INV mechanisms to FOI capability constructs

| INV mechanism (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994, 2005; Coviello, 2006) | FOI capability construct (Stage 3) | What FTRO must produce |
|---|---|---|
| Resource mobilisation across borders | Cross-border financial-operational capability | Integrated settlement, liquidity, and financing routines across markets |
| Alternative governance / internalisation choices | International regulatory-adaptation capability | Ability to satisfy foreign compliance and onboarding regimes |
| Network relationships as resource substitutes | International network-mobilisation capability | Credibility and coordination with foreign customers, suppliers, financiers |
| Entrepreneurial opportunity perception and enactment | International opportunity-enactment capability | Rapid operationalisation of foreign-market opportunities |
| Technology as enabler of speed | *(Not a capability)* — speed is Stage 4 outcome | FTRO and capabilities explain variance in entry speed |

This mapping ensures the study **extends INV theory with precise constructs** rather than claiming extension while importing unrelated variables.

### 5.2 What "extending INV theory" means — and what it does not mean

**The study claims:**

> INV theory presumes cross-border resource mobilisation but does not specify how resource-constrained emerging-market SMEs assemble the **financial infrastructure** that INV-type internationalisation requires. The FOI Framework supplies that specification through FTRO and four cross-border capabilities.

**The study does not claim:**

- that INV theory is wrong or obsolete;
- that every African SME is an INV (later internationalisers and stalled cases are included);
- that FinTech adoption alone creates INV outcomes; or
- that FTRO replaces network theory, liability-of-foreignness arguments, or institutional theory.

Reduced liability of foreignness and outsidership (Zaheer, 1995; Johanson & Vahlne, 2009) operates as an **intermediate mechanism** within the capability-to-outcome link — lower credibility, compliance, information, and transaction disadvantages — not as a terminal outcome or a substitute for capability constructs.

## 6. Why African technology-enabled service SMEs require INV grounding

Context is not a backdrop; it is a **theoretical selector** (Welter, 2011).

Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa host Africa's most developed FinTech ecosystems, so the **resource portfolio is present in all cases**. They differ in regulatory architecture, payment-system structure, and institutional depth, supplying cross-national variation for contingency propositions P5 and P6. Three features make INV grounding especially appropriate in this setting:

1. **Survival logic favours early foreign reach.** For many technology-enabled service SMEs, domestic markets are insufficient for scale; foreign customers are not a late-stage option but an early strategic necessity. That is the INV population.

2. **Financial infrastructure is not ambient.** Where institutions are thin, FinTech substitutes for missing market-supporting intermediaries — making the **orchestration mechanism** (how substitutes are assembled into capability) observable in sharper relief than in OECD contexts where INV's presumptions hold by default.

3. **Heterogeneity among similar adopters is maximised.** Firms embedded in the same ecosystems with access to the same FinTech services diverge in international outcomes. INV theory expects heterogeneity in mobilisation and network configuration; direct-effect FinTech models treat that heterogeneity as error variance. INV grounding converts observed variance into **theoretical signal**.

The study therefore includes **stalled and retrenched internationalisers** as theoretically essential cases. Without them, the design selects on success and FTRO becomes unfalsifiable. INV theory, read honestly, has always encompassed failed and arrested internationalisation attempts — not only celebrated born globals.

## 7. Alternative internationalisation anchors considered and set aside

| Alternative anchor | Why it is insufficient as primary grounding | Role in this study |
|---|---|---|
| **Uppsala / incremental models** | Calibrated to slow commitment deepening; under-theorises early rapid entry and financial-infrastructural obstacles | Background for later internationalisers; liability of outsidership in outcome link |
| **Network theory (Johanson & Vahlne, 2009)** | Explains opportunity access and trust but not FinTech resource configuration | Embedded in network-mobilisation capability |
| **Liability of foreignness (Zaheer, 1995)** | Explains cost of foreignness, not capability formation | Intermediate mechanism in Stage 3→4 |
| **Digital affordances (Autio et al., 2018)** | Ecosystem-level; does not theorise firm-level FinTech assembly | Context for digital entrepreneurship |
| **Generic digital capability (Joensuu-Salo & Matalamäki, 2023)** | Outcome competence, not process; not FinTech-specific | Organisational readiness condition (P5) |
| **Direct-effect FinTech models** | Association without mechanism; cannot explain null effects and adopters' heterogeneity | What this study moves beyond |

INV is the **primary anchor** because it matches the population, the outcome pattern, and the unresolved presumption the study addresses. ROT/FTRO is the **mechanism layer** because INV names what must happen but not how FinTech resources become the capabilities that make it happen.

## 8. Proposition-level implications of INV grounding

INV grounding disciplines the six propositions:

- **P1 (Resource foundation):** Portfolio endowment enables orchestration — consistent with INV's emphasis on resource access, but measured as functional fit rather than ownership.
- **P2 (Capability formation):** FTRO generates the four capabilities INV's resource and network mechanisms presuppose — this is the core INV extension.
- **P3 (Serial mediation):** FinTech adoption does not directly produce INV outcomes; FTRO and capabilities mediate — consistent with Oviatt and McDougall's (2005) insistence that technology enables speed through intervening processes, not by itself.
- **P4 (Complementarity):** Integrated FinTech configurations outperform isolated tools — the bundling logic INV's governance narrative implies but does not operationalise for financial services.
- **P5 (Organisational contingency):** Readiness conditions portfolio-to-FTRO conversion — absorptive capacity and digital capability as prerequisites for mobilisation.
- **P6 (Institutional contingency):** Institutions condition capability-to-outcome conversion — including non-monotonic void effects that INV's developed-market origins could not surface.

## 9. Anticipated objections and responses

**Objection 1: "Not all your cases are INVs. Why INV grounding?"**

**Response.** INV grounding does not require every case to meet a born-global threshold. It requires that the **internationalisation pattern under investigation** — early, rapid, or attempted-rapid cross-border engagement by resource-constrained firms — is the pattern INV theory was built to explain. Later internationalisers are analysed as accelerated expansion; stalled cases test whether weak FTRO predicts weak or arrested outcomes. INV is the anchor for the ** explanandum**, not a label applied only to successful cases.

**Objection 2: "You are smuggling RBV/ROT in under the INV banner."**

**Response.** The division of labour is explicit. INV supplies the internationalisation architecture; ROT supplies the conversion mechanism. The study's contribution to international entrepreneurship is precisely the **connection** INV has not made: how FinTech orchestration generates the capabilities INV-type internationalisation presumes. That is extension, not smuggling.

**Objection 3: "Digital entrepreneurship already covers technology-enabled internationalisation."**

**Response.** Digital entrepreneurship covers platforms and affordances at ecosystem level. This study's phenomenon is **FinTech-specific financial infrastructure** at firm level — settlement, credit, compliance, trust — the subset digital entrepreneurship has not theorised for user SMEs. INV grounding is what keeps the study in international entrepreneurship rather than generic technology adoption research.

**Objection 4: "INV theory is dated — it predates FinTech."**

**Response.** Correct — which is why the study **extends** rather than **applies** INV. Theories are not invalidated by technologies that postdate them; they are incomplete when new resources change the conditions of their core presumptions. FinTech makes INV's undisclosed financial presumption visible and empirically urgent in African markets.

**Objection 5: "Why not ground the study in dynamic capabilities instead?"**

**Response.** Dynamic capabilities (Teece, 2007) explain reconfiguration under change but do not specify the **resource domain** (FinTech), the **purpose** (cross-border expansion), or the **internationalisation outcome architecture** (INV pattern). FTRO may exhibit dynamic-capability properties; that is an **empirical question**, not a definitional assumption. INV remains the internationalisation anchor; ROT remains the process anchor.

## 10. Conclusion: the precise claim

INV theory is the correct theoretical grounding for this study because:

1. **Phenomenon fit** — the sampled firms are resource-constrained technology-enabled service SMEs whose internationalisation logic is early or attempted-early foreign engagement, not slow incremental commitment.
2. **Outcome architecture** — INV defines what must be explained (speed, scope, intensity, persistence through mobilisation, governance, and networks) with correct construct hygiene (speed as outcome, not capability).
3. **Identified gap** — INV presumes cross-border transactability and resource mobilisation but is silent on how emerging-market SMEs assemble financial infrastructure when it is not ambient.
4. **FinTech as institutional substitute** — in African markets, FinTech is the primary means by which that presumption can be satisfied, making orchestration — not adoption — the theoretically decisive variable.
5. **Complementarity with ROT** — INV names the pattern; FTRO explains the mechanism; together they form the FOI Framework without reduplicating or contradicting either tradition.

The study's contribution to international entrepreneurship can therefore be stated with precision:

> **The FOI Framework supplies the capability-generation mechanism that INV theory presumes but has not specified — explaining how resource-constrained African SMEs orchestrate FinTech resources into the cross-border capabilities that produce, accelerate, or fail to sustain INV-type internationalisation outcomes.**
