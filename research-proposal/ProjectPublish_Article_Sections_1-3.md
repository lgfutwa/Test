## Title

**Orchestrating FinTech for foreign markets: How African SMEs convert financial technology into internationalisation capabilities**

## Abstract

Research on financial technology (FinTech) and small and medium enterprise (SME) internationalisation predominantly models FinTech as an enabling antecedent with a direct effect on international outcomes, leaving unexplained why firms with access to similar technologies internationalise so differently. Integrating resource orchestration theory with international new venture (INV) theory, we develop and empirically refine the FinTech-Orchestrated Internationalisation framework, which proposes that internationalisation outcomes depend on FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO): the managerial process of structuring, bundling, and leveraging complementary FinTech resources — spanning transaction and settlement, financing and liquidity, identity and compliance, and trust and data-infrastructure functions — into cross-border capabilities. We conduct an abductive multiple-case study of technology-enabled service SMEs in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa, reconstructing FinTech decisions across foreign-market-entry episodes through semi-structured interviews and documentary evidence. [Findings sentence 1: summarise the dominant orchestration patterns identified.] [Findings sentence 2: summarise how orchestration, organisational readiness, and institutional conditions jointly explained outcome differences.] The study shifts the theoretical focus of FinTech–internationalisation research from technology adoption to capability creation, extends resource orchestration theory to externally sourced digital financial resources, and supplies INV theory with the capability-generation mechanism it presumes but does not specify. For managers and policymakers, the results identify which FinTech configurations — not which technologies — support entry into and persistence in foreign markets.

**Keywords:** FinTech resource orchestration; SME internationalisation; international new ventures; resource orchestration theory; Sub-Saharan Africa; emerging markets

## 1. Introduction

African SMEs face a paradox of plenty. The continent hosts some of the world's most dynamic financial-technology ecosystems: mobile money has reached mass adoption with documented effects on financial access (Suri & Jack, 2016; GSMA, 2024), digital lenders extend credit where collateral-based banking does not (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, Brohi, & Nasim, 2021), and regulatory technology automates the compliance functions that make cross-border business possible (Arner, Barberis, & Buckley, 2017). Yet SME participation in international markets remains thin, financing constraints remain the binding obstacle to smaller firms' growth (Beck & Demirgüç-Kunt, 2006; International Finance Corporation, 2017), and — most puzzling for managers and policymakers alike — firms with access to the *same* FinTech ecosystem achieve starkly different international outcomes. For the SME founder deciding which payment gateway, credit facility, or compliance tool to invest in ahead of a first foreign market entry, the pressing question is not whether FinTech matters, but which configurations of FinTech convert into foreign revenue and which do not.

The scholarly state of play cannot yet answer that question. A rapidly growing literature demonstrates that FinTech and digital technologies are positively associated with SME financing access, efficiency, and internationalisation (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021; Abbasi, Alam, Du, & Huynh, 2021; Jean & Kim, 2020; Hajizamani, Zakery, & Bonyadi Naieni, 2025), and that digital affordances underpin new modes of entrepreneurial value creation and rapid internationalisation (Autio, Nambisan, Thomas, & Wright, 2018; Nambisan, Wright, & Feldman, 2019). But this work shares a common analytical structure: FinTech (or digitalisation) enters as an independent variable with a direct effect on performance or internationalisation, while the organisational process inside the firm remains a black box. The structure's limits are visible in its own results: digitalisation shows no direct performance effect among already-internationalised SMEs (Joensuu-Salo, Sorama, Viljamaa, & Varamäki, 2018), digital platforms create risks that *reduce* the internationalisation scope of international new ventures (Jean et al., 2020), and the wave of systematic reviews published between 2023 and 2025 — spanning digital firms (Yang, Bai, Chen, & Rong, 2025), digital platform-based firms (Li, Surana, Chavan, Kano, Schotter, & Chirico, 2025), and SMEs (Anwar, Li, Al-Omush, & Al-Nimer, 2023; Bargoni, Ferraris, Vilamová, & Wan Hussain, 2024; Hajizamani et al., 2025) — converges on the same diagnosis: the field is fragmented, theoretically narrow, and lacking integrative frameworks that explain capability development. The conversation has moved from *whether* digital technologies matter for internationalisation to *how* they support it, without yet specifying a FinTech-specific organisational mechanism. Direct-effect models cannot accommodate these findings; a mechanism-based account is required.

Our point of difference is to specify that mechanism. We argue that individual FinTech resources are necessary but insufficient for internationalisation: most are externally available, transaction-priced services that any competitor can adopt, so they cannot individually satisfy the conditions for competitive advantage (Barney, 1991). What differentiates firms is **FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO)** — the managerial process, grounded in resource orchestration theory (Sirmon, Hitt, & Ireland, 2007; Sirmon, Hitt, Ireland, & Gilbert, 2011), through which an SME structures a portfolio of complementary FinTech resources, bundles them into integrated cross-border routines, and leverages the resulting capabilities in specific foreign markets. FTRO, we propose, generates the cross-border financial-operational, regulatory-adaptation, network-mobilisation, and opportunity-enactment capabilities that international new venture theory presumes young resource-constrained firms can somehow assemble (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994, 2005; Coviello, 2006) but whose formation it does not explain. In short: where prior work asks *whether* FinTech enables internationalisation, we ask *how* and *under what conditions* FinTech resources become internationalisation capabilities.

We examine this question through an abductive multiple-case study of technology-enabled service SMEs headquartered in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa — three institutionally distinct FinTech ecosystems — reconstructing the FinTech decisions taken across each firm's foreign-market-entry episodes and comparing rapidly internationalising, later-internationalising, and stalled cases. The African setting is of theoretical, not merely empirical, import: where market-supporting institutions are weak or absent (Khanna & Palepu, 1997; North, 1990), FinTech functions as an institutional substitute, making the orchestration mechanism — and its institutional contingencies — observable in sharper relief than in developed-market settings. [Findings paragraph: two to three sentences summarising the central findings once analysis is complete.]

The study makes three contributions. First, to FinTech research, it reconceptualises FinTech from an adoption variable into a configurable portfolio of four functional resource classes whose value depends on purposeful orchestration. Second, it extends resource orchestration theory (Sirmon et al., 2011) into a new domain — externally sourced digital financial services deployed for cross-border expansion by emerging-market SMEs — and treats the framework's dynamic-capability status as an empirical question rather than an assumption (Teece, 2007). Third, it supplies international entrepreneurship with the capability-generation mechanism that links digital financial resources to INV-type internationalisation, thereby explaining heterogeneous outcomes among similar adopters — the phenomenon direct-effect models leave anomalous (Cavusgil & Knight, 2015; Knight & Liesch, 2016; Zahra, 2021).

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the theoretical framework and six propositions. Section 3 describes the methodology. Section 4 presents the findings, Section 5 discusses their theoretical and managerial implications, and Section 6 concludes with limitations and future research.

## 2. Theoretical background and propositions

### 2.1 Rapid SME internationalisation and its unexplained resource base

Internationalisation research long portrayed foreign expansion as incremental: firms accumulate experiential market knowledge and deepen commitments gradually, entering psychically close markets first (Johanson & Vahlne, 1977), with the central obstacle later reframed as outsidership relative to relevant business networks (Johanson & Vahlne, 2009; Vahlne & Johanson, 2017). International new venture theory arose to explain the firms this account could not: ventures international at or near founding, which compete abroad by internalising some transactions, relying on alternative governance structures to access resources, establishing foreign location advantages, and controlling unique resources (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994). Subsequent work established that early internationalisers succeed through innovation cultures and distinctive organisational capabilities rather than tangible resource stocks (Knight & Cavusgil, 2004; Cavusgil & Knight, 2015), that internationalisation speed is an *outcome* — enabled by technology, mediated by entrepreneurial perception, moderated by knowledge and networks (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005) — and that network relationships substitute for the resources young firms lack (Coviello, 2006).

Two gaps in this tradition motivate our study. First, INV theory presumes that resource-constrained firms can mobilise resources across borders but does not explain how emerging-market SMEs assemble the financial infrastructure — payments, credit, compliance, and trust mechanisms — that cross-border operation requires; the theory predates FinTech, and the digital turn in international entrepreneurship has engaged platforms and affordances broadly (Autio et al., 2018; Nambisan, 2017; Nambisan et al., 2019) but not FinTech-specific resource configuration. Second, the liabilities of foreignness and outsidership — the costs foreign entrants bear relative to embedded local rivals (Zaheer, 1995; Johanson & Vahlne, 2009) — are most acute precisely where institutional support is thinnest, yet the mechanisms by which digital financial tools mitigate these liabilities remain undertheorised.

### 2.2 Resource orchestration: from possession to process

The resource-based view grounds advantage in valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable resources (Barney, 1991) but is famously silent on managerial action. Resource orchestration theory opens this black box: managers create value by *structuring* the resource portfolio (acquiring, accumulating, divesting), *bundling* resources into capabilities (stabilising, enriching, pioneering), and *leveraging* those capabilities in the marketplace (mobilising, coordinating, deploying), with orchestration requirements varying across firm scope, life-cycle stage, and managerial level (Sirmon et al., 2007, 2011). The dynamic-capabilities perspective adds that sustained advantage under change requires sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring (Teece, 2007).

Orchestration logic fits the FinTech–internationalisation problem unusually well, for a reason the literature has not exploited: FinTech resources are *externally available to all firms*. Payment gateways, digital credit, and compliance automation are subscription- or transaction-priced services; they cannot individually be rare or inimitable, so any firm-level advantage must arise from configuration — which is precisely what orchestration theorises. We therefore define **FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO)** as *the patterned managerial process through which an SME acquires, configures, integrates, deploys, and reconfigures complementary FinTech resources to support cross-border activities*. FTRO applies and extends resource orchestration theory in a new empirical domain; whether it constitutes a dynamic capability — requiring evidence of repeated reconfiguration under changing conditions — is treated as an empirical question.

### 2.3 FinTech as a configurable resource portfolio

FinTech scholarship has mapped the sector's ecosystem and business models (Lee & Shin, 2018), organised the research field by business functions, technologies, and institutions (Gomber, Koch, & Siering, 2017), interpreted the innovation forces transforming financial services (Gomber, Kauffman, Parker, & Weber, 2018), and identified the economic and technological determinants of where FinTech develops (Haddad & Hornuf, 2019). What this ecosystem- and country-level work leaves open is the firm-level question: how does an SME assemble FinTech services into operating capability?

For firm-level analysis we classify FinTech resources by *function* rather than technology — payments, lending, RegTech, and blockchain are not parallel categories — into four classes: (1) **transaction and settlement resources** (mobile money, wallets, gateways, foreign exchange, multi-currency settlement), which reduce the cost, delay, and failure risk of cross-border payments (Suri & Jack, 2016; GSMA, 2024); (2) **financing and liquidity resources** (digital credit, alternative scoring, marketplace lending, invoice finance), which relax the financing constraint that binds SME growth (Beck & Demirgüç-Kunt, 2006; Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021); (3) **identity, compliance, and risk resources** (digital identity, KYC/AML automation, regulatory reporting, fraud detection), the RegTech domain that reconceives compliance as a real-time data function (Arner et al., 2017); and (4) **trust, assurance, and data-infrastructure resources** (traceability, APIs, interoperable data services, smart contracts), which supply verifiable records in low-trust trading environments (Gomber et al., 2018).

Two properties of this portfolio carry the theoretical weight. The classes are *complementary* — settling foreign payments requires compliant onboarding; extending trade credit requires transaction data; winning foreign partners' trust requires verifiable records — so value arises from interactions among classes rather than from any class alone. And endowment is a matter of *availability, quality, interoperability, accessibility, and strategic fit*, not a count of tools adopted. Both properties imply that adoption measures systematically mismeasure what matters, and both motivate the first two propositions:

> **P1 (Resource foundation).** The availability, quality, interoperability, accessibility, and strategic fit of an SME's FinTech resource portfolio are positively associated with the development of FTRO.

> **P2 (Capability formation).** FTRO — structuring, bundling, and leveraging FinTech resources — generates cross-border financial-operational, regulatory-adaptation, network-mobilisation, and opportunity-enactment capabilities.

The four capabilities named in P2 are informed by INV theory's resource, governance, and network mechanisms (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994, 2005; Coviello, 2006): the ability to manage settlement, liquidity, and financing across markets (financial-operational); to interpret and satisfy foreign compliance regimes (regulatory-adaptation); to establish credibility with and coordinate foreign customers, suppliers, and financiers (network-mobilisation); and to identify and rapidly operationalise foreign opportunities (opportunity-enactment). Speed, by contrast, is an outcome to be explained (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005), not a capability firms possess.

### 2.4 From adoption to orchestration: the capability-generation gap

The empirical literature linking FinTech and digitalisation to SME internationalisation follows a dominant analytical pattern: technology enters as an antecedent; improved access, efficiency, or reach follows; internationalisation results. P2P lending increases SME access to finance, with institutional quality strengthening the effect (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021); FinTech development is associated with SME efficiency (Abbasi, Alam, Du, et al., 2021); internet platform and web capabilities improve export marketing capability and performance (Jean & Kim, 2020); and systematic reviews confirm FinTech's barrier-reducing role in SME internationalisation (Hajizamani et al., 2025) while calling for integrated theory connecting finance, technology, and capability development (Anwar et al., 2023; Bargoni et al., 2024).

The most recent consolidation of the field sharpens this diagnosis. Between 2023 and 2025, independent systematic reviews at three levels of analysis — digital firms (Yang et al., 2025), digital platform-based firms (Li et al., 2025), and SMEs (Anwar et al., 2023; Bargoni et al., 2024; Rosyidah, Sudarmiatin, & Sumarsono, 2023; Hajizamani et al., 2025) — converge on fragmentation, theoretical narrowness, and the absence of frameworks explaining how firms convert digital resources into internationalisation capabilities. Critically, the reviews closest to this study's phenomenon remain furthest from its mechanism: Hajizamani et al. (2025) treat FinTech as an enabler and stop at the firm's boundary, while Li et al. (2025) theorise platform firms as producers of digital infrastructure rather than user SMEs as configurers of financial capability. The field has moved from asking *whether* digital technologies matter to asking *how* they support internationalisation — without yet theorising FinTech Resource Orchestration.

Three anomalies expose the limits of the remaining direct-effect logic. Digitalisation shows no direct performance effect among already-internationalised SMEs (Joensuu-Salo et al., 2018), and digital capability's effects are uneven across firm sizes (Joensuu-Salo & Matalamäki, 2023). Digital platforms generate risks that reduce INVs' internationalisation scope, mitigated only by firm-level entrepreneurial orientation (Jean et al., 2020) — technology's effect depends on what the firm does with and around it. And firms in the same ecosystems with the same technology access diverge sharply in international outcomes, a heterogeneity that antecedent models must treat as error variance. Each anomaly points to the same unmodelled term: the organisational process between resource and outcome. Hence:

> **P3 (Serial mediation).** The relationship between an SME's FinTech resource portfolio and its internationalisation outcomes operates through FTRO and the resulting capabilities in sequence, rather than through direct effects of adoption.

> **P4 (Complementarity).** Strategically aligned configurations of complementary FinTech resources produce stronger internationalisation capabilities than isolated adoption of individual FinTech solutions.

Internationalisation outcomes are specified as entry speed (decision to first foreign transaction), international scope (number and diversity of markets), international intensity (foreign share of revenue, customers, or transactions), and post-entry persistence and growth. Reduced liability of foreignness and outsidership (Zaheer, 1995; Johanson & Vahlne, 2009) operates as an intermediate mechanism, not a terminal outcome.

### 2.5 Contingencies: organisational readiness and institutional conditions

Orchestration does not operate uniformly, and contextualising its operation is itself a theoretical task (Welter, 2011). Inside the firm, absorptive capacity — the ability to recognise, assimilate, and apply external knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) — conditions whether an SME can identify which FinTech resources fit its internationalisation problem and integrate them into routines; digital capability supports performance and growth but is systematically scarcer in smaller firms (Joensuu-Salo & Matalamäki, 2023); and leadership attention and slack determine whether orchestration is sustained. Hence:

> **P5 (Organisational contingency).** Absorptive capacity, digital capability, leadership commitment, and financial slack strengthen the conversion of the FinTech resource portfolio into FTRO.

Outside the firm, emerging markets are marked by institutional voids — absent or weak market-supporting intermediaries (Khanna & Palepu, 1997) — and by institutions that set transaction costs and enforcement quality (North, 1990). Regulatory fragmentation across African jurisdictions, variable infrastructure reliability, and cybersecurity exposure shape whether orchestrated capabilities can be deployed across borders. Critically, voids cut both ways: they *raise* FinTech's value as an institutional substitute (mobile money substituting for weak banking rails; Suri & Jack, 2016) while, at the extreme, preventing deployment altogether — so the institutional relationship is plausibly non-monotonic, and positive institutional-quality moderation found in OECD settings (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021) cannot simply be extrapolated to African ones. Hence:

> **P6 (Institutional contingency).** Institutional conditions shape the conversion of orchestrated capabilities into internationalisation outcomes: payment interoperability, regulatory coherence, and infrastructure reliability strengthen it; severe fragmentation and infrastructure deficiency weaken it; and moderate institutional voids may increase the value of FinTech as an institutional substitute.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Research design and context

Because the research question concerns *processes* — sequences of structuring, bundling, and leveraging decisions and their consequences — rather than variance in adoption levels, we adopted a qualitative multiple-case study design, the preferred strategy for "how" questions about contemporary processes beyond the researcher's control (Yin, 2018), executed abductively through systematic combining: the FOI framework and propositions P1–P6 served as an initial analytical template that was confronted with, and redirected by, case evidence (Dubois & Gadde, 2002). Multiple cases enable replication logic — literal replications where similar orchestration yields similar outcomes, theoretical replications where contrasting orchestration yields predictably contrasting outcomes — and yield better-grounded theory than single cases (Eisenhardt, 1989; Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). The unit of analysis is the SME; the embedded unit is the foreign-market-entry episode, permitting within-case comparison across entries as well as cross-case comparison.

The empirical context — technology-enabled service SMEs headquartered in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa — was selected for theoretical rather than convenience reasons. These are Africa's three most developed FinTech ecosystems, yet they differ sharply in regulatory architecture, payment-system interoperability, and institutional depth, supplying the cross-context variation P5 and P6 require; and because FinTech there substitutes for weak market-supporting institutions (Khanna & Palepu, 1997), the orchestration mechanism is observable in sharper relief than in developed markets, giving the context theoretical import beyond its empirical novelty.

### 3.2 Sampling and cases

We used purposive, criterion-based sampling with a maximum-variation logic (Eisenhardt, 1989). Eligible firms employed 10–250 people, had operated for at least three years, were headquartered in one of the three countries, had undertaken international activity, and used at least one FinTech resource in cross-border operations. Cases were then selected to vary on the dimensions the framework predicts should matter: country; portfolio breadth across the four resource classes; apparent orchestration maturity; internationalisation speed (rapid internationalisers, operationalised as first foreign revenue within three years of founding, consistent with born-global conventions [Knight & Cavusgil, 2004], versus later internationalisers); and outcome (sustained versus stalled or retrenched internationalisation). Including stalled cases guards against sampling on success: if orchestration explains outcomes, its weakness should be observable in less successful firms (theoretical replication; Yin, 2018).

The final sample comprised [n] SMEs ([k] per country) and [m] informants — founders, chief executives, finance leads, and heads of international business with direct knowledge of both FinTech decisions and internationalisation episodes; where possible two informants per firm provided within-case triangulation. Sample adequacy was governed by information power — sufficiency rises with the specificity of aim, sample, and theory (Malterud, Siersma, & Guassora, 2016) — and by a pre-specified stopping rule in place of loose saturation claims (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Saunders et al., 2018): data collection continued, in increments of one case per country, until the last two cases per country generated no new proposition-relevant codes and every proposition was supported, qualified, or disconfirmed by evidence from at least two cases per country. The target range of nine to twelve cases sits within the four-to-ten-case band recommended for multi-case theory building (Eisenhardt, 1989) and is consistent with empirical evidence that focused interview studies reach adequacy within nine to seventeen interviews (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022). ### 3.3 Data collection

Primary data were semi-structured interviews of 60–75 minutes, conducted online, audio-recorded, and transcribed with consent. The protocol was organised around the framework's stages and used two techniques to strengthen evidence quality: *episode-based reconstruction*, in which informants walked chronologically through each foreign-market entry and the FinTech decisions attached to it, anchoring accounts in events rather than impressions and mitigating retrospective bias; and *neutral, non-presuming wording*, so that no question assumed a technology had been adopted or had helped (e.g., "What, if anything, changed in how foreign partners dealt with you?"). The protocol was pilot-tested with two interviews — one rapid and one later internationaliser — and revised before main fieldwork; pilot data were retained only because revisions proved immaterial. [Adjust to actual pilot outcome.] Secondary data (company documentation, press coverage, provider documentation of named tools, public filings) served to triangulate interview claims and to verify internationalisation timelines independently of recall.

### 3.4 Data analysis

Analysis followed codebook thematic analysis in the template-analysis tradition (King, 2012), which is designed for the abductive situation of an a priori theoretical template refined against data, and proceeded in five steps supported by [ATLAS.ti/NVivo] for data management and audit-trail documentation. First, familiarisation and per-case memos. Second, within-case process reconstruction: a chronological narrative of each firm's market-entry episodes and associated FinTech decisions, validated against secondary data. Third, template coding: a priori codes derived from the framework (structuring, bundling, leveraging; the four capabilities; the four outcomes; organisational and institutional conditions) were applied deductively. Fourth, inductive extension: segments the template could not accommodate received new codes, the template was revised, and earlier transcripts were recoded — the locus of abductive discovery. Fifth, cross-case pattern matching: case-ordered matrices compared orchestration patterns, capabilities, and outcomes across cases and countries; predicted patterns from P1–P6 were matched against observed patterns with explicit attention to negative and deviant cases (strong portfolios with weak outcomes and the converse), following explanation-building logic (Yin, 2018). Findings are reported through a first-order/second-order data structure (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013). A second coder independently coded approximately 20% of transcripts; disagreements were resolved through documented discussion and codebook revision, the aim being coding transparency and defensibility rather than inter-rater statistics.

### 3.5 Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness followed Lincoln and Guba's (1985) criteria. *Credibility* was pursued through data triangulation (multiple informants where available; interviews crossed with documents; three countries), member checking of interpreted case summaries rather than raw transcripts, and staged peer debriefing. *Transferability* rests on thick description of each case's sector, FinTech stack, internationalisation history, and institutional setting, together with the explicit boundary conditions of the population studied. *Dependability* is supported by a documented audit trail of sampling decisions, protocol revisions, codebook versions, and the stopping-rule decision. *Confirmability* was pursued through a reflexivity journal and through reporting deviant-case evidence alongside confirming evidence, exposing the framework to genuine disconfirmation. Ethical approval was granted by the Gordon Institute of Business Science Research Ethics Committee; participation was voluntary and anonymised, with pseudonymised reporting, encrypted storage, and compliance with applicable data-protection law including POPIA.
