## Abstract

Research on FinTech and small and medium enterprise (SME) internationalisation predominantly models FinTech as an enabling antecedent with a direct effect on internationalisation or firm performance, leaving the intervening organisational mechanism unspecified. This proposal addresses that capability-generation gap. Drawing on Resource Orchestration Theory (Sirmon et al., 2007, 2011) and International New Venture (INV) theory (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994), it develops the FinTech-Orchestrated Internationalisation (FOI) Framework, which proposes that internationalisation outcomes depend not on FinTech adoption per se, but on FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO): the managerial process through which SMEs structure, bundle, and leverage complementary FinTech resources — transaction and settlement, financing and liquidity, identity and compliance, and trust and data-infrastructure resources — into cross-border internationalisation capabilities. The study asks how rapidly internationalising, technology-enabled service SMEs in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa orchestrate FinTech resources; what capabilities this orchestration generates; how those capabilities shape the speed, scope, intensity, and persistence of internationalisation; and how organisational readiness and institutional conditions contextualise the process. An abductive, qualitative multiple-case study (nine to twelve SMEs across the three countries) using semi-structured interviews and documentary evidence, analysed through codebook thematic analysis with cross-case pattern matching, will refine six theoretical propositions. The study extends Resource Orchestration Theory into the FinTech-enabled internationalisation context, supplements INV theory with an explicit capability-generation mechanism, and offers actionable guidance to SMEs, FinTech providers, and policymakers.

## Keywords

FinTech Resource Orchestration; SME internationalisation; International New Venture theory; Resource Orchestration Theory; African SMEs; emerging markets

# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 Background: The Business Problem

Small and medium enterprises are central to African economic development, yet their participation in international markets remains disproportionately low relative to their domestic weight. Access to finance is consistently identified as the leading constraint on SME growth in emerging markets: financing obstacles are both more severe for smaller firms and more damaging to their growth than for large firms (Beck & Demirgüç-Kunt, 2006). The International Finance Corporation (2017) estimates a formal MSME financing gap in Sub-Saharan Africa of approximately USD 331 billion, and cross-border activity compounds the problem by adding currency conversion costs, settlement delays, fragmented regulatory requirements, and weak contract-enforcement institutions (Khanna & Palepu, 1997; North, 1990).

At the same time, Africa has become a globally significant site of financial-technology innovation. Mobile money has achieved mass adoption across East and West Africa, with documented welfare and financial-access effects at household level (Suri & Jack, 2016) and continued rapid growth in registered accounts and transaction values (GSMA, 2024). Digital lending platforms extend credit using alternative data where collateral-based bank lending is inaccessible (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021), regulatory technology (RegTech) automates compliance functions such as customer onboarding and anti-money-laundering screening (Arner et al., 2017), and distributed-ledger applications offer verifiable transaction records in low-trust trading environments (Gomber et al., 2018).

The business problem, then, is a paradox of plenty: African SMEs now operate amid an unusually rich FinTech ecosystem, yet many still fail to internationalise or to sustain international operations. Understanding why some SMEs convert this ecosystem into international growth while others — with access to the same technologies — do not, is of direct practical importance to SME managers, FinTech providers, and policymakers designing digital-trade and financial-inclusion policy.

## 1.2 The Academic Problem and Theoretical Hook

The academic problem is distinct from the business problem and must be stated separately. Existing studies predominantly examine the association between FinTech adoption (or digital technology adoption) and internationalisation outcomes, treating FinTech as an enabling antecedent while leaving the organisational mechanism through which FinTech resources are transformed into internationalisation capabilities largely unspecified.

Three literatures converge on this omission without resolving it:

**First, INV theory explains why early and rapid internationalisation is possible but is silent on financial technologies as a resource class.** Oviatt and McDougall (1994) established that ventures can be international from inception when they internalise some transactions, rely on alternative governance structures to access resources, establish foreign location advantages, and control unique resources; later work modelled the speed of internationalisation as a function of enabling technology, mediating entrepreneurial perception, and moderating knowledge and networks (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005; Coviello, 2006; Knight & Cavusgil, 2004). This body of work presumes that firms can mobilise resources across borders but does not theorise how resource-constrained emerging-market SMEs assemble the *financial* infrastructure — payments, credit, compliance, and trust mechanisms — that cross-border operation requires.

**Second, the resource-based tradition shows that resources alone do not confer advantage, but its orchestration logic has not been extended to FinTech.** Barney (1991) grounds competitive advantage in valuable, rare, inimitable resources, and Resource Orchestration Theory specifies the managerial actions — structuring, bundling, and leveraging — through which resource endowments become value-creating capabilities (Sirmon et al., 2007, 2011). No study has yet applied this orchestration logic to the portfolio of FinTech resources available to internationalising SMEs.

**Third, the FinTech and digital-internationalisation literatures document enabling effects but not capability formation.** FinTech research maps ecosystems, business models, and country-level development (Lee & Shin, 2018; Gomber et al., 2017; Haddad & Hornuf, 2019) and demonstrates firm-level financing and efficiency effects (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021; Abbasi, Alam, Du, et al., 2021). Digital-internationalisation research shows that platform and internet capabilities improve export outcomes while introducing new risks (Jean & Kim, 2020; Jean et al., 2020), and the wave of systematic reviews published between 2023 and 2025 — spanning digital firms (Yang et al., 2025), digital platform-based firms (Li et al., 2025), and SMEs (Anwar et al., 2023; Bargoni et al., 2024; Hajizamani et al., 2025) — converges on fragmentation and the absence of integrated frameworks explaining capability development. Notably, digitalisation does not uniformly improve performance — among already-internationalised Finnish SMEs, digitalisation showed no direct performance effect (Joensuu-Salo et al., 2018) — which is precisely the heterogeneity a direct-effect model cannot explain and an orchestration model can.

The theoretical hook of this study is therefore the introduction of **FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO)** — the managerial process through which an SME structures, bundles, and leverages complementary FinTech resources into cross-border internationalisation capabilities — as the missing mechanism linking FinTech resources to INV-type internationalisation. This shifts the explanatory focus from *whether* FinTech is associated with internationalisation to *how* and *under what conditions* FinTech resources become internationalisation capabilities, and it explains why firms with similar FinTech access achieve dissimilar international outcomes.

## 1.3 Purpose Statement, Research Questions, and Scope

The purpose of this qualitative multiple-case study is to explain how rapidly internationalising, technology-enabled service SMEs in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa orchestrate FinTech resources into internationalisation capabilities, and how organisational and institutional conditions shape that process.

**Primary research question:**

> How do African technology-enabled service SMEs orchestrate FinTech resources to develop the capabilities required for rapid and sustained internationalisation?

**Sub-questions:**

- **RQ1 (Orchestration processes):** How do these SMEs structure, bundle, and leverage FinTech resources — across transaction and settlement, financing and liquidity, identity and compliance, and trust and data-infrastructure functions — in the course of internationalising?
- **RQ2 (Capability formation):** What cross-border internationalisation capabilities are generated through these orchestration processes?
- **RQ3 (Outcomes):** How do these capabilities influence the speed, scope, intensity, and persistence of the SMEs' internationalisation?
- **RQ4 (Contextual contingencies):** How do organisational readiness (e.g., absorptive capacity, digital capability, leadership commitment) and institutional conditions (e.g., regulatory fragmentation, infrastructure reliability, institutional voids) shape FTRO and its outcomes?

**Research objectives:**

1. Reconstruct, within each case, the sequence of FinTech structuring, bundling, and leveraging decisions taken during internationalisation.
2. Identify and characterise the cross-border capabilities these orchestration processes generate.
3. Trace how those capabilities relate to observed internationalisation outcomes (entry speed, market scope, international intensity, and post-entry persistence).
4. Identify the organisational and institutional conditions that enable or constrain FTRO, refining the FOI Framework's propositions.

**Scope and boundary conditions.** The study is bounded to technology-enabled service SMEs (10–250 employees) headquartered in Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa that have undertaken international activity. These three markets are selected because they host Africa's most developed — yet institutionally distinct — FinTech ecosystems, providing the cross-country variation needed for RQ4. Claims are made about this population, not about African SMEs in general. The study distinguishes *rapidly internationalising* SMEs (first foreign revenue within three years of founding, consistent with common born-global operationalisations; Knight & Cavusgil, 2004) from later internationalisers, and deliberately includes both, as well as firms whose internationalisation stalled, to avoid sampling on success.

The next chapter builds the theoretical foundation for this inquiry and develops the FOI Framework and its propositions.

# Chapter 2: Literature Review and Theoretical Framework

## 2.1 Introduction and Conceptual Roadmap

This review integrates three streams — SME internationalisation theory, Resource Orchestration Theory, and FinTech research — to develop the FOI Framework. The argument proceeds in five moves. Section 2.2 establishes that INV theory explains rapid internationalisation through resource and network mechanisms but does not theorise financial technologies. Section 2.3 introduces Resource Orchestration Theory as the managerial mechanism the FinTech-internationalisation literature lacks. Section 2.4 reconceptualises FinTech as a configurable portfolio of four functional resource classes. Section 2.5 critically synthesises the empirical evidence and specifies the capability-generation gap. Section 2.6 identifies the contextual contingencies characteristic of African markets. Section 2.7 integrates these elements into the FOI Framework and six propositions.

The framework's causal logic is:

```text

          ↓

          ↓

          ↓

```

with organisational readiness conditioning the resources-to-FTRO link and institutional conditions conditioning the capabilities-to-outcomes link.

## 2.2 SME Internationalisation: From Incremental Stages to International New Ventures

The Uppsala model characterises internationalisation as an incremental commitment process driven by experiential learning, in which firms enter psychically close markets first and deepen commitment as market knowledge accumulates (Johanson & Vahlne, 1977). Its 2009 revision reframes the central obstacle as the liability of *outsidership* relative to relevant business networks, rather than psychic distance alone (Johanson & Vahlne, 2009), and its later evolution treats internationalisation as a general process of firm evolution under uncertainty (Vahlne & Johanson, 2017).

INV theory arose to explain a phenomenon the incremental view could not: firms that are international at or near founding. Oviatt and McDougall (1994) identify four necessary and sufficient elements of the INV — internalisation of some transactions, strong reliance on alternative governance structures to access resources, foreign location advantages, and control over unique resources. Two refinements matter for this study. First, speed of internationalisation is an *outcome* to be explained — modelled as enabled by technology, mediated by entrepreneurial opportunity perception, and moderated by knowledge and network relationships (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005) — not a capability firms possess. Second, network relationships are the principal substitute for the resources young firms lack: they supply market knowledge, credibility, and access, and their dynamics shape internationalisation patterns (Coviello, 2006). Born-global research adds that innovation culture and specific organisational capabilities, rather than tangible resource stocks, underpin early internationalisers' performance (Knight & Cavusgil, 2004; Cavusgil & Knight, 2015; Knight & Liesch, 2016).

Two limitations motivate this study. First, INV theory presumes resource mobilisation across borders but does not explain how emerging-market SMEs assemble the financial infrastructure this requires; the theory predates the FinTech phenomenon, and subsequent international-entrepreneurship research has engaged digital platforms and digital affordances broadly (Autio et al., 2018; Nambisan et al., 2019; Zahra, 2021) but not FinTech-specific resource configuration. Second, the liability of foreignness — the costs foreign firms bear relative to local competitors (Zaheer, 1995) — and the liability of outsidership are especially acute for African SMEs facing thin institutional support, yet the mechanisms by which digital financial tools mitigate these liabilities remain undertheorised.

A terminological caution guides the study's usage throughout: *resource leverage, speed-to-market, and network coordination are not canonical INV constructs*. This proposal therefore anchors its capability constructs in Resource Orchestration Theory (Section 2.3) and treats INV theory as the explanation of the internationalisation pattern those capabilities produce.

## 2.3 Resource Orchestration Theory: The Missing Managerial Mechanism

The resource-based view holds that sustained advantage derives from resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable (Barney, 1991). Its recognised limitation is silence on managerial action: possessing resources does not itself create value. Sirmon et al. (2007) open this black box by specifying resource management as structuring the resource portfolio (acquiring, accumulating, divesting), bundling resources into capabilities (stabilising, enriching, pioneering), and leveraging capabilities in the marketplace (mobilising, coordinating, deploying). Sirmon et al. (2011) integrate this with asset-orchestration research into Resource Orchestration Theory (ROT), showing that orchestration requirements vary with firm scope, life-cycle stage, and managerial level. The dynamic-capabilities perspective supplies the complementary insight that firms must sense opportunities, seize them, and reconfigure assets as environments shift (Teece, 2007).

ROT is well suited to the FinTech-internationalisation problem for three reasons. First, FinTech resources are *externally available to all firms* — most are subscription or transaction-priced services — so they cannot satisfy the rarity condition individually; any advantage must arise from firm-specific configuration, which is precisely what orchestration theorises. Second, ROT's three processes map naturally onto observable FinTech decisions: selecting providers (structuring), integrating payments with credit and compliance tools into workflows (bundling), and deploying the integrated stack in specific foreign markets (leveraging). Third, ROT explicitly accommodates life-cycle and context variation (Sirmon et al., 2011), which the cross-country design exploits.

Applied to this study's phenomenon, **FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO)** is defined as: *the patterned managerial process through which an SME acquires, configures, integrates, deploys, and reconfigures complementary FinTech resources to support cross-border activities.* FTRO is presented as a context-specific application and extension of ROT. It is not automatically claimed as a dynamic capability; that stronger claim requires evidence of repeated reconfiguration under changing conditions (Teece, 2007) and is treated as an empirical question.

## 2.4 FinTech as a Configurable Resource Portfolio

FinTech research has matured from ecosystem description — startups, incumbents, technology developers, government, and customers interacting across payments, lending, wealth management, and insurance business models (Lee & Shin, 2018) — through conceptual organisation of the field by business functions, technologies, and institutions (Gomber et al., 2017), to analysis of the innovation and transformation forces reshaping financial services (Gomber et al., 2018) and of the economic and technological determinants of where FinTech develops (Haddad & Hornuf, 2019). This literature treats FinTech at ecosystem, industry, or country level; the firm-level question of how an SME assembles FinTech services into operating capability is largely unaddressed.

For firm-level analysis, this study classifies FinTech resources by *function* rather than by technology, because payments, lending, RegTech, and blockchain are not parallel categories (the first two are services, RegTech is an application domain, and blockchain is an underlying architecture):

1. **Transaction and settlement resources.** Mobile money, digital wallets, payment gateways, foreign-exchange services, and multi-currency settlement. These reduce the cost, delay, and failure risk of cross-border payment flows (Suri & Jack, 2016; GSMA, 2024).
2. **Financing and liquidity resources.** Digital credit, alternative credit scoring, peer-to-peer and marketplace lending, embedded finance, and invoice or supply-chain finance. Evidence from OECD countries shows P2P lending FinTechs increase SME access to finance, with institutional quality strengthening the effect (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021), and FinTech development is associated with SME efficiency gains (Abbasi, Alam, Du, et al., 2021).
3. **Identity, compliance, and risk resources.** Digital identity, know-your-customer (KYC) and anti-money-laundering (AML) automation, regulatory reporting, and fraud detection — the RegTech domain, which reconceptualises compliance as a real-time, data-driven function (Arner et al., 2017).
4. **Trust, assurance, and data-infrastructure resources.** Traceability systems, application programming interfaces (APIs), interoperable data services, smart contracts, and, where commercially relevant, distributed ledgers that provide verifiable transaction records in low-trust environments (Gomber et al., 2018).

Two properties of this portfolio drive the theoretical argument. First, the resource classes are *complementary*: settling foreign-customer payments requires compliant onboarding; extending trade credit requires transaction data; establishing trust with foreign partners requires verifiable records. Value arises from interaction among classes, not from any class alone. Second, endowment must be assessed on *availability, quality, interoperability, accessibility, and strategic fit* — not on a count of technologies adopted. Both properties imply that adoption-based measures systematically mismeasure what matters, motivating the orchestration lens.

The dominant analytical pattern across these studies is linear: FinTech (or digitalisation) → improved financial access or lower transaction costs → better performance or internationalisation. Most studies do not explicitly claim that technology itself generates competitive advantage; rather, they model FinTech as an independent variable with a direct effect, leaving the intervening organisational mechanism unspecified. The 2023–2025 review wave sharpens this diagnosis: independent consolidations at digital-firm, platform-firm, and SME levels converge on fragmentation and the absence of frameworks explaining *how* firms convert digital resources into internationalisation capabilities — the field has moved from asking *whether* digital technologies matter to asking *how* they support internationalisation, without yet theorising FinTech Resource Orchestration. Four questions therefore remain insufficiently answered:

1. How do SMEs strategically combine multiple FinTech solutions into integrated internationalisation capabilities?
2. Why do firms using similar FinTech technologies experience different internationalisation outcomes?
3. What organisational process transforms FinTech resources into the capabilities INV theory presumes?
4. How do organisational and institutional conditions shape the effectiveness of FinTech-enabled internationalisation?

These four questions constitute the **capability-generation gap** that the FOI Framework addresses, and they map directly onto RQ1–RQ4.

## 2.6 Contextual Contingencies in African Markets

FTRO does not operate uniformly. Consistent with the contextualisation turn in entrepreneurship research (Welter, 2011), the framework specifies two classes of contingency. In this qualitative design they are treated as *contextual conditions to be traced within and across cases*, not as statistical moderators.

**Organisational readiness.** Absorptive capacity — the ability to recognise, assimilate, and apply external knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) — conditions whether an SME can identify which FinTech resources fit its internationalisation problem and integrate them into workflows. Digital capability similarly supports performance and growth in SMEs, and smaller firms tend to possess less of it (Joensuu-Salo & Matalamäki, 2023). Leadership commitment and financial slack determine whether orchestration receives sustained attention and investment. Firms stronger on these dimensions should extract more capability from the same resource portfolio.

**Institutional conditions.** Emerging markets are characterised by institutional voids — absent or weak market-supporting intermediaries (Khanna & Palepu, 1997) — and by institutions that structure transaction costs and enforcement (North, 1990). Regulatory fragmentation across African jurisdictions (divergent KYC, AML, data-protection, and licensing regimes), variable digital-infrastructure reliability, and cybersecurity exposure all shape whether orchestrated capabilities can actually be deployed across borders. Importantly, institutional voids are not uniformly negative for this phenomenon: they can *increase* the value of FinTech as an institutional substitute (e.g., mobile money substituting for weak banking rails), while severe voids may prevent deployment altogether. The relationship is therefore plausibly non-monotonic — a matter the cross-country design is positioned to explore, and one reason institutional quality's positive moderation of FinTech-finance effects in OECD settings (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021) cannot simply be extrapolated to African settings.

## 2.7 The FinTech-Orchestrated Internationalisation (FOI) Framework and Propositions

Integrating Sections 2.2–2.6, the FOI Framework comprises four stages and two contingencies.

**Stage 1 — FinTech resource portfolio.** The four functional resource classes of Section 2.4, assessed on availability, quality, interoperability, accessibility, and strategic fit. Individual FinTech resources are necessary but insufficient: they are raw materials, not capabilities.

**Stage 2 — FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO).** The framework's theoretical core, comprising ROT's three processes (Sirmon et al., 2011) applied to the FinTech portfolio:

- *Structuring:* acquiring, accumulating, and divesting FinTech resources — e.g., selecting payment providers and compliance tools for strategic fit rather than adopting technology for its own sake.
- *Bundling:* integrating resources into capabilities — e.g., combining a payment gateway, invoice finance, and automated KYC into a single cross-border order-to-cash process; value arises from interaction among resources.
- *Leveraging:* mobilising, coordinating, and deploying the bundled capability in specific foreign markets — e.g., reconfiguring the FinTech stack (local gateway, regional compliance tool, cross-border financing) on each new market entry.

**Stage 3 — Cross-border internationalisation capabilities.** Orchestration generates four capabilities, informed by INV theory's resource, governance, and network mechanisms (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994, 2005; Coviello, 2006):

1. *Cross-border financial-operational capability:* managing settlement, liquidity, financing, and financial information across markets.
2. *International regulatory-adaptation capability:* interpreting, satisfying, and adjusting to foreign compliance and onboarding requirements.
3. *International network-mobilisation capability:* establishing credibility and coordinating customers, suppliers, financiers, and platform partners across borders.
4. *International opportunity-enactment capability:* identifying, evaluating, and rapidly operationalising foreign-market opportunities.

Where sampled firms internationalised early, these capabilities explain INV/born-global patterns (Knight & Cavusgil, 2004); for later internationalisers they explain accelerated post-founding expansion. Speed is an outcome, not a capability.

**Stage 4 — Internationalisation outcomes.** Four observable outcomes: *entry speed* (time from internationalisation decision to first foreign transaction), *international scope* (number and diversity of foreign markets), *international intensity* (share of revenue, customers, or transactions abroad), and *post-entry persistence and growth*. Reduced liability of foreignness and outsidership (Zaheer, 1995; Johanson & Vahlne, 2009) is treated as an intermediate mechanism — lower credibility, compliance, information, and transaction disadvantages — rather than a terminal outcome.

**Contingencies.** Organisational readiness conditions the portfolio-to-FTRO link; institutional conditions condition the capabilities-to-outcomes link.

**Theoretical propositions.** Consistent with abductive case research (Yin, 2018; Dubois & Gadde, 2002), the following working propositions guide data collection and are subject to refinement, extension, or disconfirmation:

- **P1 (Resource foundation).** The availability, quality, interoperability, accessibility, and strategic fit of an SME's FinTech resource portfolio are positively associated with the development of FTRO.
- **P2 (Capability formation).** FTRO — structuring, bundling, and leveraging FinTech resources — generates cross-border financial-operational, regulatory-adaptation, network-mobilisation, and opportunity-enactment capabilities.
- **P3 (Serial mediation).** The relationship between an SME's FinTech resource portfolio and its internationalisation outcomes operates through FTRO and the resulting capabilities in sequence, rather than through direct effects of adoption.
- **P4 (Complementarity).** Strategically aligned configurations of complementary FinTech resources produce stronger internationalisation capabilities than isolated adoption of individual FinTech solutions.
- **P5 (Organisational contingency).** Absorptive capacity, digital capability, leadership commitment, and financial slack strengthen the conversion of the FinTech resource portfolio into FTRO.
- **P6 (Institutional contingency).** Institutional conditions shape the conversion of orchestrated capabilities into internationalisation outcomes: payment interoperability, regulatory coherence, and infrastructure reliability strengthen it; severe fragmentation and infrastructure deficiency weaken it; and moderate institutional voids may increase the value of FinTech as an institutional substitute.

**Theoretical contribution.** The FOI Framework makes three contributions. To FinTech research, it reconceptualises FinTech from an adoption variable into a configurable resource portfolio whose value depends on purposeful orchestration. To Resource Orchestration Theory, it extends structuring–bundling–leveraging into a new empirical domain — externally sourced digital financial services deployed for cross-border expansion by emerging-market SMEs. To international entrepreneurship, it supplies the capability-generation mechanism that INV theory presumes but does not specify, explaining heterogeneous internationalisation outcomes among firms with similar technology access. The alignment of business problem, academic problem, framework, and research questions constitutes the proposal's golden thread (Appendix 1).

# Chapter 3: Research Methodology and Design

## 3.1 Research Philosophy and Abductive Approach

The study adopts an **interpretivist** philosophy: orchestration decisions, capability formation, and institutional constraints are apprehended through the meanings managers attach to them, and these meanings are context-dependent across the three countries studied. Interpretivism suits "how" questions about processes unfolding inside firms, where the phenomenon cannot be separated from its context (Yin, 2018).

The reasoning strategy is **abductive**, operationalised as *systematic combining* (Dubois & Gadde, 2002): the FOI Framework and propositions P1–P6 provide an initial analytical frame; fieldwork confronts the frame with empirical surprises; and the frame is iteratively redirected as data, theory, and case evidence are matched against one another. This is the appropriate logic for a study that neither tests hypotheses deductively nor builds theory from a blank slate inductively, but refines an articulated framework.

## 3.2 Research Design: Qualitative Multiple-Case Study

A **qualitative multiple-case study** is selected for three explicit reasons rather than by default:

1. **Process questions require process evidence.** RQ1–RQ3 concern sequences of managerial decisions (structuring → bundling → leveraging) and their consequences. Case studies are the preferred strategy for "how" questions about contemporary processes over which the researcher has no control (Yin, 2018); surveys measuring adoption levels cannot observe orchestration sequences.
2. **Multiple cases enable replication logic and stronger theory.** Each case serves as an analytical replication: literal replications (similar orchestration patterns producing similar outcomes) and theoretical replications (contrasting patterns producing contrasting outcomes for predictable reasons) discipline proposition refinement (Yin, 2018; Eisenhardt, 1989). Multi-case designs yield better-grounded, more generalisable theory than single cases (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007).
3. **The contingency questions require variation.** RQ4 demands variation in institutional context (three countries) and organisational readiness (heterogeneous firms), which a multiple-case design supplies by construction.

The unit of analysis is the **individual SME**; the embedded unit for process reconstruction is the **market-entry episode** (each foreign-market entry, with its associated FinTech decisions), allowing within-case comparison across episodes as well as cross-case comparison.

## 3.3 Population, Sampling, and Case Selection

**Population.** Technology-enabled service SMEs (10–250 employees; operational for at least three years) headquartered in Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa that have undertaken international activity (exporting services, serving foreign clients, or operating abroad) and use at least one FinTech resource in cross-border operations.

**Sampling strategy.** Purposive, criterion-based sampling with a **maximum-variation logic** (Eisenhardt, 1989). Because the study aims to explain heterogeneity in outcomes, cases are selected to vary on the dimensions the framework says should matter:

- country and regulatory setting (Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa);
- FinTech portfolio configuration (breadth across the four resource classes);
- apparent orchestration maturity (single-tool users through integrated-stack operators);
- internationalisation speed (rapid internationalisers — first foreign revenue within three years of founding — and later internationalisers);
- internationalisation outcome (sustained international operations *and* stalled or retrenched attempts).

Including stalled internationalisers is a deliberate guard against success bias and selection-on-the-dependent-variable: if orchestration explains outcomes, weak or absent orchestration should be observable in less successful cases (theoretical replication).

**Sample size and adequacy.** The target is **nine to twelve SMEs (three to four per country), with one to two informants per SME (12–20 interviews)**. This is justified on three grounds rather than asserted: (a) it permits at least one contrast pair (rapid vs. stalled) per country, the minimum structure replication logic requires (Yin, 2018); (b) it falls within the 4–10 case range recommended for multi-case theory building (Eisenhardt, 1989); and (c) it is consistent with evidence that qualitative interview studies with focused aims and information-rich informants typically reach adequacy within 9–17 interviews (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022). Adequacy is assessed through **information power** — sample sufficiency increases with the specificity of the aim, sample, and theory (Malterud et al., 2016) — rather than through a mechanical saturation claim.

**Operationalising thematic sufficiency.** Rather than invoking "saturation" loosely (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Saunders et al., 2018), the study specifies a decision rule in advance: after the initial nine cases are coded, data collection continues in increments of one case per country until two conditions hold: (1) no new codes relevant to P1–P6 have been generated by the last two cases in that country, and (2) each proposition is supported, qualified, or disconfirmed by evidence from at least two cases per country. The decision, and the evidence for it, will be documented in the audit trail.

**Informant selection.** Founders, chief executives, finance leads, or heads of international business with direct knowledge of both FinTech decisions and internationalisation episodes. Where possible, two informants per firm (e.g., founder plus finance lead) provide within-case triangulation.

## 3.4 Data Collection

**Primary data: semi-structured interviews** (60–75 minutes, online via Teams/Zoom, audio-recorded and transcribed with consent). The interview guide (Appendix 2) is structured around the framework's stages and uses two techniques to improve evidence quality:

- **Episode-based reconstruction:** informants walk through each foreign-market entry chronologically, anchoring accounts in specific events rather than general impressions, which mitigates retrospective and social-desirability bias.
- **Neutral, non-presuming wording:** questions never presuppose that a technology was adopted or that its effect was positive (e.g., "What, if anything, changed in how foreign partners dealt with you?" rather than "Has blockchain improved trust?").

The guide will be **pilot-tested with two interviews** (one rapid internationaliser, one later internationaliser); pilot learnings will be documented and the guide revised before main fieldwork. Pilot interviews will be included in the dataset only if the guide changes immaterially.

**Secondary data:** company websites, product documentation, press coverage, regulatory filings where public, and provider-side documentation of the FinTech tools named in interviews. Secondary data serve two functions: triangulating interview claims (e.g., verifying markets served and tools used) and establishing timelines independently of recall.

## 3.5 Data Analysis

Analysis follows **codebook thematic analysis in the template-analysis tradition** (King, 2012), which is designed for exactly this situation: an a priori theoretical template refined against data. This resolves the incoherence of combining grounded-theory vocabulary (theoretical sampling, axial coding) with Braun and Clarke's approach; grounded-theory terminology is not used. The procedure, supported by ATLAS.ti or NVivo for data management and audit-trail documentation (the software manages data; the researcher performs the analysis):

1. **Familiarisation.** Repeated reading of transcripts against recordings; case memos written per firm.
2. **Within-case process reconstruction.** For each firm, a chronological narrative of internationalisation episodes and associated FinTech decisions, validated against secondary data.
3. **Template coding.** An initial template derived from the FOI Framework: structuring, bundling, leveraging (ROT); the four capability constructs; the four outcome dimensions; organisational and institutional conditions. A priori codes are applied deductively.
4. **Inductive extension.** Data segments that the template cannot accommodate receive new inductive codes; the template is revised, and earlier transcripts are recoded against the revised template. Emergent capabilities, orchestration practices, or contingencies are expected and welcomed — this is where the abductive contribution arises.
5. **Cross-case pattern matching.** Case-ordered matrices compare orchestration patterns, capabilities, and outcomes across cases and countries; predicted patterns from P1–P6 are matched against observed patterns, with explicit attention to **negative and deviant cases** (e.g., strong portfolios with weak outcomes; weak portfolios with strong outcomes) using explanation-building logic (Yin, 2018).
6. **Data structure and reporting.** Following Gioia et al. (2013), the final report will present a transparent data structure (first-order concepts → second-order themes → aggregate dimensions) with verbatim evidence, linking findings to each research question and proposition.

**Coding validation.** A second coder (supervisor or academic peer) will independently code a purposive subsample of transcripts (approximately 20%); disagreements will be resolved through discussion and documented, with the codebook revised where disagreement reveals ambiguity. Consistent with the interpretivist stance, the aim is coding *transparency and defensibility*, not inter-rater statistics.

## 3.6 Rigour and Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness follows Lincoln and Guba (1985), with concrete mechanisms:

- **Credibility.** Data triangulation (multiple informants where possible; interviews × documents; three countries); member checking (informants receive their case summary and the interpretations attributed to their firm, not merely transcripts, for correction); peer debriefing with the supervisor at each analysis stage.
- **Transferability.** Thick description of each case's sector, FinTech stack, internationalisation history, and institutional setting, enabling readers to judge applicability; explicit boundary conditions (Section 1.3).
- **Dependability.** A documented audit trail — sampling decisions, guide revisions, codebook versions, template revisions, and the thematic-sufficiency decision — maintained in the analysis software and a research journal.
- **Confirmability.** A reflexivity journal recording the researcher's assumptions and evolving interpretations; deviant-case analysis reported alongside confirming evidence, so that the framework is genuinely exposed to disconfirmation.

## 3.7 Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval will be obtained from the GIBS Research Ethics Committee before any data collection. Key commitments (consent form in Appendix 3):

- **Informed consent:** written consent covering purpose, voluntariness, withdrawal without penalty (with destruction of withdrawn data), recording, and use of anonymised verbatim quotations.
- **Anonymity and confidentiality:** pseudonyms for firms and individuals; identifying details (named clients, unique products) redacted from quotations; the key linking identities to codes stored separately and accessibly only to the researcher and supervisor.
- **Data security:** recordings and transcripts on encrypted, password-protected university-approved storage; retention per university policy (minimum five years), then secure destruction. Cross-border data handling will comply with POPIA (South Africa) and applicable Kenyan and Nigerian data-protection law.
- **Minimising harm:** commercially sensitive questions are answerable at the level of generality the informant chooses; any question may be declined.

## 3.8 Limitations

- **Analytical, not statistical, generalisation.** Findings generalise to theory (the FOI Framework) and to similar contexts, not statistically to all African SMEs (Yin, 2018). The bounded population claim (Section 1.3) is maintained throughout.
- **Retrospective self-report.** Mitigated by episode-based reconstruction, timeline verification against secondary data, and within-case informant triangulation — but not eliminated; residual recall bias is acknowledged.
- **Access and self-selection.** Firms willing to discuss FinTech and internationalisation may differ systematically from refusers. Recruiting through multiple channels (professional networks, incubators, FinTech providers' merchant communities, LinkedIn) and deliberately recruiting stalled internationalisers reduce, but do not remove, this bias.
- **Online interviewing.** Online interviews may exclude informants with poor connectivity and can attenuate rapport. Scheduling flexibility, telephone fallback, and camera-optional practice mitigate this; the residual bias toward digitally connected firms is acknowledged — though it is less distorting here than it would be elsewhere, since digital connectivity is a scope condition of the population.
- **Researcher interpretation.** Inherent to interpretivist analysis; disciplined through the reflexivity journal, second-coder validation, and the documented audit trail.
- **Cross-sectional design.** Process is reconstructed retrospectively rather than observed longitudinally; a longitudinal follow-up is identified as future research, alongside quantitative operationalisation and testing of FTRO once the qualitative study has specified its dimensions.

## Appendix 2: Revised Interview Guide

**Research title:** FinTech-Orchestrated Internationalisation: How African Technology-Enabled Service SMEs Convert FinTech Resources into Internationalisation Capabilities
**Researcher:** 24126196 | **Interviewee role:** ______ | **Company (pseudonym):** ______ | **Country:** ______

**Section 0 — Introduction and informed consent (5–10 min).** Purpose, voluntariness, recording consent, anonymity, right to skip or stop.

**Section 1 — Firm background and internationalisation timeline (10–15 min)** *(RQ3 baseline)*

1. Briefly describe your company: core business, size, and history.
2. Walk me through your international activity from the beginning: which market first, when, and how did that come about?
3. For each market entered since: when, why that market, and through what mode?
4. Have any international activities been paused, scaled back, or abandoned? What happened?

**Section 2 — FinTech structuring: acquiring the portfolio (10–15 min)** *(RQ1)*

5. What financial tools and services does the business use to operate across borders — for payments, funding, compliance, or anything else? *(Probe each named tool: when adopted, and what problem it was meant to solve.)*
6. How did you choose between alternatives, if there were any? Who made that decision, and on what basis?
7. Have you dropped or replaced any of these tools? What prompted that?

**Section 3 — Bundling and leveraging: integration and deployment (15–20 min)** *(RQ1, RQ2)*

8. Pick your most recent (or most important) foreign-market entry. Take me through, step by step, how a foreign customer goes from first contact to paid invoice. Which tools are involved at each step, and how do they connect?
9. When you entered [market X], did you change anything about this setup? What, and why?
10. Tell me about a specific occasion when this setup was tested — a large foreign order, a compliance check, a payment failure. What happened, and what did you learn or change?
11. What can the business do internationally today that it could not do three years ago? What made that possible?

**Section 4 — Outcomes (10 min)** *(RQ3)*

12. How long did it take from deciding to enter [market X] to the first paid transaction there? What consumed most of that time?
13. Roughly what share of your revenue or customers is international today, and how has that changed?
14. What technologies or arrangements, if any, changed how foreign customers or partners dealt with you — for better or worse? Please describe a specific event and its consequences.

**Section 5 — Organisational and institutional conditions (10–15 min)** *(RQ4)*

15. What inside the firm has made adopting and integrating these tools easier or harder — skills, leadership attention, funds, systems?
16. What outside the firm — regulation, licensing, infrastructure, connectivity, security — has made cross-border use of these tools easier or harder? Please give a concrete example from a specific market.
17. If a similar firm in [other study country] tried to replicate your setup, what do you think would work differently, and why?

**Section 6 — Close (5 min)**

18. What are your future internationalisation plans, and what role, if any, do financial technologies play in them?
19. Is there anything important we have not covered?

*Closing script:* "Thank you for your time. Everything you have said will remain confidential and anonymised. I will send you a summary of how I have interpreted your firm's experience, and I would welcome your corrections."

## Appendix 4: Research Project Plan

**1. Objectives (recap).** Reconstruct FTRO processes (RQ1); identify capabilities generated (RQ2); trace capability–outcome links (RQ3); identify contextual contingencies (RQ4); refine the FOI Framework and propositions P1–P6.

**2. Milestones (months from proposal approval).**

| Phase | Tasks | Deliverable | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethics | Application with instruments | Approval letter | Month 1 |
| Pilot | 2 pilot interviews; guide revision; pilot memo | Revised guide | Month 2 |
| Recruitment | Criterion screening; case register incl. stalled internationalisers | 9–12 confirmed cases | Months 2–3 |
| Data collection | 12–20 interviews; document assembly | Transcripts; document corpus | Months 3–5 |
| Analysis | Within-case narratives; template coding; second-coder validation; cross-case matrices; sufficiency decision | Coded dataset; matrices; audit trail | Months 4–6 |
| Findings and discussion | Chapters 4–5 with Gioia-style data structure | Draft chapters | Months 6–7 |
| Finalisation | Full draft; supervisor review; submission | Final report | Month 8 |

**3. Resources.** ATLAS.ti or NVivo (data management and audit trail); encrypted storage; access to SMEs via professional networks, incubators, FinTech provider merchant communities, and alumni; regular supervision checkpoints at guide revision, codebook v1, and cross-case analysis.

**4. Risk management.**

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Recruitment delays, especially of stalled internationalisers | Multiple channels; snowballing from confirmed cases; incubator and provider introductions; per-country buffer cases |
| Ethics delay | Early, complete application with instruments attached |
| Uneven data quality | Pilot testing; episode-based protocol; secondary-data timeline verification |
| Single-informant cases | Seek second informant; weight documentary triangulation where unavailable |
| Analysis drift from framework | Supervision checkpoints tied to codebook versions; documented template revisions |
| Time overruns | Phase buffers; prioritise minimum case structure (one contrast pair per country) |

**5. Golden thread alignment.**

| Element | Alignment |
|---|---|
| Business problem | African SMEs have rich FinTech access yet heterogeneous internationalisation outcomes |
| Academic problem | The organisational mechanism converting FinTech resources into internationalisation capabilities is unspecified in INV, ROT-application, and FinTech literatures |
| Framework | FOI: portfolio → FTRO → capabilities → outcomes, under organisational and institutional contingencies |
| Research questions | RQ1–RQ4 map one-to-one onto the framework's stages and contingencies |
| Method | Abductive multiple-case design with episode-based process evidence and contrast cases |
| Analysis | Template coding against the framework with inductive extension and negative-case logic |
| Contribution | Extends ROT into FinTech-enabled internationalisation; supplies INV theory's missing capability-generation mechanism; actionable guidance for SMEs and policymakers |
