**Title:** Orchestrating FinTech for foreign markets: How African technology-enabled service SMEs convert financial technology into internationalisation capabilities

## 1. Opening statement

This research defends a single, precise claim: **internationalisation outcomes among African technology-enabled service SMEs do not follow automatically from FinTech adoption; they follow from FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO)** — the managerial process through which firms structure, bundle, and leverage complementary FinTech resources into cross-border internationalisation capabilities.

The study is necessary because the literature has moved from asking *whether* digital and financial technologies support SME internationalisation to asking *how* they do so, yet no study theorises FTRO as the organisational mechanism that converts FinTech resources into International New Venture (INV)-type capabilities (Yang, Bai, Chen, & Rong, 2025; Li, Surana, Chavan, Kano, Schotter, & Chirico, 2025; Anwar, Li, Al-Omush, & Al-Nimer, 2023; Bargoni, Ferraris, Vilamová, & Wan Hussain, 2024; Hajizamani, Zakery, & Bonyadi Naieni, 2025). The FinTech-Orchestrated Internationalisation (FOI) Framework supplies that mechanism, and an abductive qualitative multiple-case study in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa tests and refines it.

The defense rests on four pillars: (1) the problem is real and theoretically specified; (2) the framework is the appropriate theoretical response; (3) the methodology is fit for the research questions; and (4) the contribution is original, defensible, and practically consequential.

## 2. Defense of the research problem

### 2.1 Why this topic matters

**Business rationale.** African technology-enabled service SMEs face a paradox of plenty: they operate amid some of the world's most dynamic FinTech ecosystems — mobile money, digital credit, RegTech, interoperable payment rails — yet remain uneven in their ability to convert that access into sustained foreign revenue. Managers invest in payment gateways, lending platforms, and compliance tools without clear guidance on *which configurations* of these resources support international entry and persistence. Policymakers promoting digital trade and financial inclusion need to know which institutional conditions allow SME FinTech investment to translate into export capability, not merely domestic financial access.

**Academic rationale.** The academic problem is not "FinTech helps SMEs" — that association is increasingly well established. The academic problem is that **the organisational process between FinTech resources and internationalisation outcomes remains unspecified**. Empirical studies predominantly model FinTech or digitalisation as an enabling antecedent with a direct effect on performance or internationalisation (Abbasi, Alam, Brohi, et al., 2021; Jean & Kim, 2020; Hajizamani et al., 2025). Three findings within that same literature undermine the direct-effect logic: digitalisation shows no direct performance effect among already-internationalised SMEs (Joensuu-Salo, Sorama, Viljamaa, & Varamäki, 2018); digital platforms generate risks that reduce INV internationalisation scope (Jean, Kim, & Cavusgil, 2020); and firms with similar technology access diverge sharply in international outcomes — heterogeneity that antecedent models must treat as error variance.

The 2023–2025 wave of systematic reviews independently converges on fragmentation and the absence of integrative frameworks explaining capability development (Yang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Anwar et al., 2023). The field has learned to ask *how*; this study answers *how*, specifically for FinTech, specifically at firm level, specifically in institutionally thin emerging markets.

### 2.2 Why Africa, and why these three countries

The African setting is **theoretically necessary**, not merely empirically convenient. In developed markets, the financial infrastructure of cross-border trade — settlement, credit, compliance, trust — is ambient; INV theory could take it for granted (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994). In Sub-Saharan Africa it cannot: financing constraints bind SMEs disproportionately (Beck & Demirgüç-Kunt, 2006), the formal MSME financing gap is large (International Finance Corporation, 2017), and institutional voids raise transaction costs (Khanna & Palepu, 1997; North, 1990). FinTech functions as an **institutional substitute** in such settings (Suri & Jack, 2016; Haddad & Hornuf, 2019), which makes the orchestration mechanism — how firms assemble substitutes into operating capability — observable in sharper relief than in OECD contexts.

Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa are selected because they host Africa's three most developed FinTech ecosystems (so the resource portfolio is present in all cases) while differing in regulatory architecture, payment-system structure, and institutional depth (so contingency propositions P5 and P6 can be examined cross-nationally). Claims are bounded to **technology-enabled service SMEs** in these three markets — not to "African SMEs" in general.

### 2.3 Why not a simpler research question?

A simpler question — "Does FinTech adoption improve SME internationalisation?" — would be empirically tractable but **theoretically redundant**. The literature has already answered it in the affirmative at the association level. A mechanism study is required because:

1. Association does not explain **heterogeneity** among similar adopters.
2. Association does not identify **which combinations** of FinTech resources matter.
3. Association does not specify **organisational and institutional conditions** under which FinTech investment pays off.

The primary research question — *how do African technology-enabled service SMEs orchestrate FinTech resources to develop the capabilities required for rapid and sustained internationalisation?* — is therefore not an evasion of empirical difficulty; it is the question the literature has recently learned to ask and has not yet answered.

## 3. Defense of the theoretical framework

### 3.1 Why Resource Orchestration Theory (ROT)

ROT is not one lens among many; it is **the appropriate lens** for this phenomenon because FinTech resources fail the rarity condition of the resource-based view at the individual-resource level. Payment gateways, digital wallets, and compliance automation are externally supplied, subscription-priced services available to any firm. Under Barney's (1991) logic, no single FinTech tool can be a source of sustained advantage. Advantage, if it exists, must reside in **firm-specific configuration** — selection, integration, and deployment of complementary resources — which is precisely what Sirmon, Hitt, and Ireland's (2007, 2011) structuring–bundling–leveraging processes theorise.

Alternative theories were considered and set aside for stated reasons:

| Alternative | Why it is insufficient alone | Role in this study |
|---|---|---|
| Technology acceptance / UTAUT | Explains adoption intention, not post-adoption capability formation or international outcomes | Subsumed under structuring (acquisition choices) |
| Dynamic capabilities (Teece, 2007) | Explains reconfiguration under change but not the specific resource domain or cross-border purpose | FTRO may evidence dynamic-capability properties; treated as empirical question |
| Digital affordances (Autio et al., 2018) | Ecosystem-level; does not theorise firm-level FinTech configuration | Background for digital context |
| Direct-effect FinTech models | Cannot explain null effects and outcome heterogeneity | What this study moves beyond |

### 3.2 Why International New Venture (INV) theory

INV theory supplies the **internationalisation pattern** to be explained — early or rapid foreign engagement through resource mobilisation, alternative governance, and network coordination (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994, 2005; Coviello, 2006; Knight & Cavusgil, 2004) — but presumes a financial resource base it never specifies. The FOI Framework does not replace INV theory; it **completes** it by identifying how resource-constrained emerging-market SMEs assemble the financial infrastructure INV-type internationalisation requires.

**Construct hygiene:** Speed-to-market is treated as an **outcome** (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005), not a capability. "Resource leverage," "speed-to-market," and "network coordination" are not presented as canonical INV constructs; the study's capability constructs are defined in ROT-informed terms (financial-operational, regulatory-adaptation, network-mobilisation, opportunity-enactment).

### 3.3 Why FinTech Resource Orchestration (FTRO) as the core mechanism

FTRO is defined as the patterned managerial process of acquiring, configuring, integrating, deploying, and reconfiguring complementary FinTech resources for cross-border activities. It is novel **in domain** (externally sourced digital financial services), **in purpose** (cross-border expansion), and **in setting** (institutionally thin emerging markets) — not in process categories, which are drawn faithfully from ROT.

The framework's four-stage logic is defended as follows:

```text
FinTech resource portfolio  →  FTRO  →  Cross-border capabilities  →  Internationalisation outcomes
         (P1, P4)              (P2)           (P3)                    (speed, scope, intensity, persistence)
```

Organisational readiness conditions the portfolio-to-FTRO link (P5); institutional conditions condition the capability-to-outcome link (P6), including a plausibly **non-monotonic** role for institutional voids (substitution value versus deployment blockage).

### 3.4 Defense of the six propositions

| Proposition | What it claims | Why it is testable in this design |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** Resource foundation | Portfolio quality/fit → FTRO development | Episode interviews reconstruct acquisition and selection decisions |
| **P2** Capability formation | FTRO → four cross-border capabilities | Template codes map structuring/bundling/leveraging to capability evidence |
| **P3** Serial mediation | Portfolio → FTRO → capabilities → outcomes (not direct adoption effects) | Cross-case comparison of high-adoption/low-outcome vs. low-adoption/high-outcome firms |
| **P4** Complementarity | Integrated configurations outperform isolated tools | Within-case bundling evidence; contrast narrow-integrated vs. broad-disconnected portfolios |
| **P5** Organisational contingency | Readiness strengthens portfolio-to-FTRO conversion | Variation in absorptive capacity, digital capability, leadership, slack across cases |
| **P6** Institutional contingency | Institutions shape capability-to-outcome conversion | Three-country comparison; voids as substitute vs. blocker |

Propositions are **working propositions** in Yin's (2018) sense: they guide data collection and pattern matching and may be supported, qualified, extended, or disconfirmed.

## 4. Defense of the methodology

### 4.1 Why qualitative multiple-case study

The research questions are **process questions** (how firms structure, bundle, leverage; how capabilities form; how contingencies operate). Surveys measuring adoption levels cannot observe orchestration sequences. Experiments are infeasible and would strip institutional embeddedness. A qualitative multiple-case study is the preferred strategy for contemporary "how" questions where phenomenon and context are inseparable (Yin, 2018; Eisenhardt, 1989).

Multiple cases enable **replication logic**: literal replications (similar orchestration → similar outcomes) and theoretical replications (weak orchestration → weak outcomes for predictable reasons). Including **stalled internationalisers** is essential: without them, the design selects on success and FTRO becomes unfalsifiable.

### 4.2 Why abductive systematic combining

Pure deduction would test a framework whose constructs (FTRO, four capabilities) require empirical elaboration. Pure induction would ignore the theoretical foundation Chapter 2 provides. **Systematic combining** (Dubois & Gadde, 2002) holds the FOI Framework as an evolving template confronted with field evidence — appropriate for a study that extends theory rather than merely applying or discarding it.

### 4.3 Why template analysis (not grounded theory)

The original proposal mixed grounded-theory vocabulary (theoretical sampling, axial coding) with thematic analysis — an incoherence this design corrects. **Codebook thematic analysis in the template-analysis tradition** (King, 2012) matches the abductive logic: a priori codes from the framework, inductive extension where data exceed the template, recoding after template revision, cross-case pattern matching with negative-case analysis.

### 4.4 Sample size and adequacy

Nine to twelve SMEs (three to four per country), twelve to twenty interviews, is justified by:

1. **Replication architecture** — at least one contrast pair (sustained vs. stalled) per country (Yin, 2018).
2. **Theory-building convention** — within Eisenhardt's (1989) four-to-ten-case range, extended modestly for cross-national coverage.
3. **Information power** — narrow aim, information-rich informants, strong theoretical backdrop raise adequacy without mechanical saturation claims (Malterud, Siersma, & Guassora, 2016; Hennink & Kaiser, 2022).

A **pre-specified two-condition stopping rule** replaces loose saturation rhetoric: no new proposition-relevant codes from the last two cases per country, and every proposition addressed by at least two cases per country.

### 4.5 Trustworthiness

Credibility (triangulation, member checking of interpreted summaries, peer debriefing), transferability (thick description, explicit boundaries), dependability (audit trail), and confirmability (reflexivity journal, deviant-case reporting) follow Lincoln and Guba (1985). Generalisation is **analytical** — to the FOI Framework and comparable contexts — not statistical.

## 5. Anticipated examiner challenges and responses

### 5.1 Theoretical challenges

**Challenge 1: "You are merely relabelling digital capability or dynamic capabilities as FTRO."**

**Response.** Digital capability (Joensuu-Salo & Matalamäki, 2023) is a firm-level competence outcome, not a process description. FTRO specifies *how* that competence is built in the FinTech domain — through structuring, bundling, and leveraging identifiable resource classes. Dynamic capabilities (Teece, 2007) require evidence of repeated reconfiguration; the study does not assume FTRO is a dynamic capability but tests whether reconfiguration across market entries occurs. If informants describe one-off adoption without integration, that is evidence *against* strong FTRO, not confirmation of an existing construct.

**Challenge 2: "Why FinTech specifically? Why not digital platforms or digitalisation broadly?"**

**Response.** Digitalisation is the umbrella; FinTech is the **financial infrastructure** subset that addresses the specific frictions of cross-border trade — settlement, credit, compliance, verifiable records. INV theory's unexplained presumption concerns financial transactability, not connectivity per se. Platform-firm reviews (Li et al., 2025) theorise infrastructure producers; this study theorises **user SMEs** configuring financial services. The functional four-class taxonomy (transaction, financing, compliance, trust/data) is FinTech-specific and would be mis-specified for generic digitalisation.

**Challenge 3: "Your propositions sound like hypotheses for quantitative testing. Why qualitative?"**

**Response.** The propositions are **theoretical statements** guiding pattern matching, not falsifiable parametric hypotheses. Qualitative research is appropriate for *opening* the black box — identifying orchestration practices, capability manifestations, and contingent conditions — that a later quantitative study could operationalise and test. The study's contribution is conceptual and processual refinement; P3's serial-mediation claim is illustrated and qualified in cases, not estimated with path coefficients.

**Challenge 4: "Institutional voids cannot be both good and bad for FinTech. Pick one."**

**Response.** P6 explicitly theorises a **non-monotonic** relationship. Moderate voids increase FinTech's value as institutional substitute (mobile money for weak banking rails; alternative scoring for absent credit bureaus). Severe voids (unreliable connectivity, incoherent licensing) prevent deployment regardless of orchestration skill. The three-country design is positioned to observe both mechanisms; the proposition is falsified if voids uniformly help or uniformly harm across cases.

### 5.2 Methodological challenges

**Challenge 5: "Nine to twelve cases cannot represent African SMEs."**

**Response.** Agreed — and the study does not claim statistical representation. It claims **analytical generalisation** to theory (Yin, 2018): refined constructs, qualified propositions, and bounded conditions for firms resembling the sampled population (technology-enabled service SMEs in FinTech-rich, institutionally diverse African markets). Thick description and explicit boundaries enable readers to judge transferability.

**Challenge 6: "Interview data are retrospective and biased."**

**Response.** Retrospection is a residual limitation, not an unmitigated flaw. Mitigations: episode-based reconstruction anchoring accounts in dateable events; documentary timeline verification; two informants per firm where possible; member checking of interpreted summaries. The design does not claim to observe orchestration in real time; it claims to reconstruct orchestration histories with disciplined, triangulated evidence.

**Challenge 7: "You will only find successful firms willing to talk."**

**Response.** The sampling plan **requires** stalled or retrenched internationalisers as theoretical replication cases. Recruitment uses multiple channels (incubators, FinTech provider communities, professional networks, snowballing) specifically to reach less visible failure cases. Success bias is acknowledged as residual risk, not ignored.

**Challenge 8: "Online interviews exclude the disconnected."**

**Response.** Acknowledged. Digital connectivity is a **scope condition** of the population (technology-enabled service SMEs using FinTech in cross-border operations). Online conduct is a practical necessity for three-country elite informant access; telephone fallback and flexible scheduling mitigate exclusion within that population.

### 5.3 Contribution and originality challenges

**Challenge 9: "Hajizamani et al. (2025) already proposed an integrated FinTech–SME internationalisation framework. What is new?"**

**Response.** Hajizamani et al. (2025) confirm FinTech as an enabler and propose a multidimensional integrative framework at the **review level** — barriers, mediators, outcomes — but do not theorise the **intra-firm orchestration process** through which multiple FinTech resources become capabilities. The FOI Framework's novelty is FTRO as a mechanism, ROT-grounded process categories, four functional resource classes with complementarity logic, and six testable propositions linked to RQ1–RQ4. The relationship is **extension**, not duplication: their review identifies the gap; this study fills it.

**Challenge 10: "This is a consulting report dressed as theory."**

**Response.** The practical implications (configuration-level guidance for managers, interoperability for providers, institutional design for policymakers) follow from theoretical mechanism, not the reverse. The contribution is judged by whether FTRO advances ROT, completes INV's resource presumption, and explains heterogeneity that direct-effect models cannot — criteria that hold regardless of managerial utility.

## 6. Defense of contribution

### 6.1 To FinTech research

Reconceptualises FinTech from an adoption variable into a **configurable portfolio** of four functional resource classes whose value depends on orchestration — addressing the firm-level *use* gap that ecosystem, field, and determinants research leaves open (Lee & Shin, 2018; Gomber, Koch, & Siering, 2017; Haddad & Hornuf, 2019).

### 6.2 To Resource Orchestration Theory

Extends structuring–bundling–leveraging into externally sourced digital financial services deployed for cross-border expansion in emerging markets — a domain ROT has not previously addressed, with explicit attention to why rarity fails at the resource level.

### 6.3 To international entrepreneurship

Supplies the capability-generation mechanism INV theory presumes: how resource-constrained SMEs assemble financial infrastructure for INV-type internationalisation, converting outcome heterogeneity among similar adopters from error variance into theoretical signal.

### 6.4 Practical significance

Managers receive **configuration-level** guidance (which combinations, in what sequence, under what internal conditions). FinTech providers learn that **interoperability and integration** are competitive parameters, not optional features. Policymakers gain evidence on **institutional conditions** — payment interoperability, regulatory coherence, infrastructure reliability — under which SME FinTech investment converts into export capability.

---

## 7. Defense of feasibility and ethics

**Feasibility.** The study is cross-sectional with retrospective reconstruction, feasible within a single research cycle. Recruitment channels (incubators, alumni networks, FinTech merchant communities, LinkedIn) are established in the three target markets. The interview protocol is pilot-tested; analysis software and supervision checkpoints are specified.

**Ethics.** GIBS Research Ethics Committee approval precedes data collection. Informed consent, anonymisation (with attention to small visible communities), encrypted storage, POPIA compliance, and voluntary withdrawal without penalty are specified. Commercial sensitivity is managed through informant-controlled generality of responses.

**Limitations owned.** Analytical not statistical generalisation; retrospective self-report; access and self-selection bias; cross-sectional horizon. Each limitation states mitigations and residual risk and identifies future research (longitudinal panel, quantitative operationalisation of FTRO, comparative extension to other African markets and sectors).

## 9. Closing defense statement

This research is defensible because it answers a question the field has recently learned to ask, with a mechanism the field has not yet specified, using a method fit for process evidence, in a context where that mechanism is theoretically visible, with propositions exposed to disconfirmation through negative and deviant cases.

The FinTech-Orchestrated Internationalisation Framework does not claim that FinTech always works, or that orchestration always succeeds. It claims that **when** African technology-enabled service SMEs internationalise effectively amid rich FinTech ecosystems, they do so by orchestrating complementary financial technologies into cross-border capabilities — and that **when** they fail despite access to the same tools, weak, blocked, or absent orchestration is a principled explanation worth investigating.

That claim is modest enough to be wrong in identifiable ways, and ambitious enough to matter for theory, practice, and policy. On those grounds, the research warrants approval and execution.
